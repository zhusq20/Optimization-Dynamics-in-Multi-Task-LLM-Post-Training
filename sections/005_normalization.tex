\section{Token Balancing and Response Length}
\label{sec:normalization}

\subsection{Why can token balancing change MOPD optimization?}

Equal prompt quotas do not imply equal optimization influence in MOPD: domains and teachers elicit responses of different lengths. The averaging rule therefore chooses what is balanced. GT weights every valid supervised token equally and lets long-response domains contribute more; DT fixes each domain's total weight but still favors longer responses within a domain; DR also weights responses equally within each domain. These choices can change which capabilities dominate a batch, even when the prompt mixture is unchanged.

The distinction is visible directly in the gradient. Writing $g_i=\nabla_\theta\ell_i$, the token-weighted gradient within domain $d$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_d}T_i g_i}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_d}T_i}
=\bar g_d+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_d(T,g)}{\bar T_d}.
\label{eq:weighting}
\end{equation}
The covariance term is nonzero whenever response length and gradient are related. DT retains this term; DR removes it. Correcting GT by the target-to-observed domain token share gives DT exactly, as in Open-MOPD \citep[Section~4.1, Eq.~(8)]{gao2026openmopddiagnosingfixingcapability}. We therefore test two linked questions: whether length weighting changes raw gradients and capability balance, and whether clipping and Adam preserve or mask that difference before it reaches the parameters. Appendix~\ref{app:normalization} gives the derivation.

\subsection{Experiment: averaging rules and capability balance}

\paragraph{Comparison.}
We use a Qwen3-1.7B student and four frozen domain teachers for mathematics, code, instruction following, and science. Three online branches use student--teacher top-64 intersection supervision (I64) and differ only in GT, DT, or DR averaging. They share initialization, teachers, prompt schedule, and optimizer settings; each update uses 16 prompts per domain. Capability is evaluated on MATH-500, a fixed LiveCodeBench subset, IFBench, and GPQA Diamond through update 500, with equal domain weights in the reported mean. Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives the training and scoring protocol.

\paragraph{Different paths lead to similar I64 endpoint means.}
Mathematics and instruction following each receive 25\% of prompts but contribute 44.05\% and 14.98\% of GT's valid tokens over the first 50 batches; GT therefore gives mathematics nearly three times the weight. DT and DR fix both domains at 25\% (Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_capability}a). This imbalance changes early trajectories, yet no rule wins consistently across seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_capability}). At update 500, four-domain means are $38.58\pm0.75$\% (DR), $38.71\pm0.80$\% (DT), and $38.64\pm0.80$\% (GT). The 0.14-point span is below the 0.75--0.80-point seed standard deviations. MATH and IFBench approach their teachers, LiveCodeBench retains a gap, and GPQA starts near its teacher; rule ranks change across tasks and checkpoints. Here token balancing changes capability trajectories while yielding similar final means.

\paragraph{Endpoint behavior depends on the objective and optimizer.}
Table~\ref{tab:completed_endpoints} extends the comparison to PG under all three averaging rules, I16 under DR, and three PG/SGD runs. These endpoints use seed 42 and are separate from the three-seed normalization summaries above. All ten configurations improve the initial four-domain mean, from 33.93\% to 38.03--39.46\%. Thus the narrow I64/Adam spread does not establish the same behavior across configurations. The SGD learning rate and deployment differ from Adam, so this comparison does not isolate a single optimizer mechanism or establish an efficiency ranking. \paragraph{The optimizer masks part of the gradient difference.}
On six fixed response banks, the mean raw-gradient cosines are 0.680 (DR--DT), 0.483 (DR--GT), and 0.822 (DT--GT); hence equal domain weights do not make DR and DT equivalent. Global clipping leaves every cosine unchanged, even though it acts very differently online: DR is clipped on all 500 updates and retains a median 9.3\% of its gradient, compared with clipping on 46.2\% of DT updates and 15.0\% of GT updates (Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_interventions}a). Applying one common saved Adam state then raises the three FP32 update cosines to 0.960, 0.939, and 0.979. Adam history therefore changes the effective update rather than merely transmitting the current clipped gradient. Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_interventions}(b) shows that it also sets the step scale.



\paragraph{Shared history attenuates local differences.}
With saved-state Adam, the three reductions' immediate held-out KL changes are close relative to bank variation (Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_interventions}c). Same-norm SGD exposes a wider, checkpoint-dependent spread: all three mean changes improve at update 100 but worsen at update 250. Clipping changes gradient magnitudes but not angles, whereas shared Adam history pulls distinct gradients toward similar updates. These interventions identify a local mechanism; they do not explain the full online trajectories, whose responses and optimizer histories evolve separately. Appendix~\ref{app:fixed_batch_normalization} gives the intervention details and raw-gradient comparisons; Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_ci} reports paired capability contrasts.
