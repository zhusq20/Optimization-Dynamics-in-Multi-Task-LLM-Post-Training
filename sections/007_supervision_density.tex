\section{Does Supervision Density Change Update Sparsity?}
\label{sec:density}

Our third study asks whether broader vocabulary supervision changes the concentration of parameter updates and whether it improves the resulting capabilities. Supervision density here refers to the vocabulary alternatives scored at each prefix. PG uses sampled-token feedback; I64 uses the student/teacher top-64 intersection; T64 and full-vocabulary reverse KL provide local comparisons. Sampled-token feedback depends on every logit through the softmax normalizer, so it need not produce sparse parameter gradients. Dense feedback can also accompany concentrated parameter changes \citep{yu2026denseupdates}.

\subsection{Matched single-teacher and joint experiments}

The online comparison uses four configurations: S-PG and S-I64 with the mathematics teacher, and M-PG and M-I64-DR with four routed teachers. Within each setting, initialization, teachers, domain-response averaging, optimizer settings, prompt schedule, and response cap are matched. Each branch generates its own on-policy responses. The joint I64 branch is reused in the first two studies. Each single-teacher update uses 64 mathematics responses; each joint update uses 16 per domain, so an update gives different mathematics exposure across settings.

The local comparison evaluates PG, I64, T64, and full vocabulary on the same two prefix banks at M-PG update 100, with identical prefix weights and saved optimizer state. These calculations measure proposed steps at a common state. The online runs measure cumulative changes and capability along their own trajectories. Full-vocabulary online training and single-teacher local comparisons are not included in the aligned evidence.

\subsection{Update concentration and learning outcomes}

\paragraph{Online sparsity.}
The joint PG and I64 cumulative changes in Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} are concentrated in BF16 under both losses. At update 100, I64 changes 4.84\% of stored coordinates versus 3.41\% for PG, a difference of 1.44 percentage points before rounding. Their 90\%-energy fractions are 2.48\% and 1.82\%, respectively. I64 therefore has broader cumulative activity at this checkpoint, while both remain concentrated. The corresponding FP32 nonzero fractions differ by 0.45 points. This is a measured trajectory difference; it does not isolate vocabulary coverage from the responses generated by each evolving student.

\paragraph{Local sparsity.}
Figure~\ref{fig:density} compares changes proposed by the same saved-state Adam calculation. Mean BF16 nonzero fractions are 0.148599\% for PG and 0.148587\% for I64 and full vocabulary; FP32 nonzero fractions are about 87.96\% throughout. The largest 1\% of coordinates carry about 8.9\% of FP32 squared step norm and all BF16 squared step norm. At this measured state, broader vocabulary supervision has very little effect on these local sparsity statistics.

The prefixes have high top-64 coverage: student probability on the teacher's top-64 tokens averages 99.9184--100\% within each bank-domain group. The local finding is limited to these prefixes and the saved state. Appendix~\ref{app:losses} reports the coverage; Appendix~\ref{app:records} provides threshold sensitivity and the zero-current-gradient control. Similar active fractions alone do not imply identical parameter changes.

\paragraph{Capability.}
Table~\ref{tab:density_capability} compares completed PG/I64 evaluations at the same update within each setting. In single-teacher training, PG leads mathematics by 2.8 points at update 100, while I64 leads by 0.6 points at update 250. In joint training at update 100, PG leads mathematics and instruction following, whereas I64 leads code and GPQA. There is no consistent capability advantage across these domains and checkpoints. \subsection{Interpretation and decision rule}

The measurements show concentrated BF16 cumulative changes under both joint losses, a broader I64 cumulative support at update 100, and nearly unchanged local sparsity under broader supervision. The single-teacher capability comparison is available, but its matching parameter geometry is needed before extending the sparsity conclusion to both settings. These one-seed observations describe effect sizes in the measured setting; they do not establish statistical equivalence or justify choosing a loss from sparsity alone.
