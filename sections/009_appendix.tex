\section{Length-Weighting Derivation}
\label{app:normalization}

Fix a rollout batch, its valid-token masks, and domain loss weights $\lambda_d$. The PG log-ratio coefficients are treated as constants during backpropagation. For $n_d=|\mathcal B_d|$, let $\bar T_d=n_d^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_d}T_i$ and $\bar g_d=n_d^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_d}g_i$. Define the empirical covariance using the same $1/n_d$ normalization:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}_d(T,g)=\frac{1}{n_d}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B_d}(T_i-\bar T_d)(g_i-\bar g_d).
\end{equation}
Expanding gives $n_d^{-1}\sum_iT_i g_i-\bar T_d\bar g_d$. Dividing by $\bar T_d$ gives Equation~\ref{eq:weighting}. GT combines the resulting domain gradients using observed token shares; DT combines them using $\lambda_d$; DR combines $\bar g_d$ using $\lambda_d$. Multiplying each domain's globally averaged terms by its target weight divided by its observed token share therefore gives DT exactly.

\subsection{Fixed-batch gradient comparison}
\label{app:fixed_batch_normalization}

At M-PG checkpoints 100 and 250, three cached response batches (seeds 1042, 1043, and 1044) each contain eight responses per domain. Responses use the training prompt format and 4,096-token response cap. For each batch, we compute each response's mean I64 gradient once at the saved BF16 model values using FP32 computation, then aggregate those gradients under DR, DT, and GT. All three averaging rules use the same responses, token masks, teacher scores, and model coordinates. Each comparison starts from the same student parameters and Adam first and second moments.

Across the six batches, the DR--DT raw-gradient cosine ranges from 0.40 to 0.91 (mean 0.68; Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_capability}b). The domain-wise covariance decomposition is checked in FP64 for all 24 batch-domain groups; the largest relative residual is $1.53\times10^{-15}$. This numerical check verifies the aggregation identity. The measured cosine differences show that response length and gradient direction are correlated in these batches.

The optimizer interventions in Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_interventions} reuse these six batches. Each aggregate gradient is globally clipped at norm 1 and applied to the same saved FP32 Adam state for that batch. The saved-state branch retains both moments; the reset-$m$ branch zeros the first moment while retaining the second moment and optimizer step counter; reset-$(m,v)$ zeros both moments while retaining the step counter. The momentum-free SGD control follows the negative gradient and is rescaled separately for DR, DT, and GT to match the FP32 Adam step norm for that averaging rule. After each FP32 update, the model parameters are cast to BF16 and evaluated on one held-out response per domain. Evaluation uses full-vocabulary reverse KL; the experiment archive also retains the I64 surrogate used for training. Each comparison applies a single optimizer step to a checkpoint copy.

\subsection{Coordinates and recorded quantities}

Training measurements separately record gradients before and after clipping, FP32 optimizer steps, and cumulative parameter changes in the FP32 master weights and BF16 model weights. Cumulative changes are measured from the same initialization. All global statistics use unique model coordinates, excluding vocabulary padding and duplicate entries for tied weights. Per-layer statistics and absolute-threshold sensitivities are retained in the experiment archive. For an absolute threshold $\tau$, sparsity is the fraction of coordinates with magnitude at most $\tau$; the figures show the fraction exceeding $\tau$. Energy concentration uses squared Euclidean norm and ranks coordinates by absolute change.

For checkpoint-based measurements, the exported checkpoint contains FP32 master values, BF16 model values, first and second moments, optimizer hyperparameters, and step counters. Student and teacher values are verified against their saved models in the same precision. Forward and backward passes evaluate these values in FP32 with TF32 disabled. All proposed steps are computed from the same saved state with a non-fused Adam implementation. The online training optimizer uses its own fused implementation.

We compare three Adam states at the same checkpoint. \emph{Saved} retains both moments and the step counter. \emph{Reset m} zeros the first moment while retaining the second moment and step counter. \emph{Fresh} zeros both moments and resets the step counter. Global norm clipping at 1 is applied before the Adam calculation, and weight decay is zero. The plotted controls use two cached response batches; the zero-gradient control is evaluated once. For the zero-gradient branch, the saved first moment continues to decay and move the master weights. Casting the saved FP32 master weights to BF16 without an optimizer step exactly reproduces the saved model. Figure~\ref{fig:thresholds} shows how the threshold changes the measured fraction of updated coordinates.

Setting the current gradient to zero while retaining the trained Adam moments changes 0.14\% of BF16 coordinates, compared with 0.15\% for PG. Resetting the first moment gives 0.05\% for PG; resetting both moments and the step counter gives 0.51\%. The parameters changed by Adam depend jointly on the current gradient, accumulated moments, and BF16 rounding. These controls supplement the update-sparsity measurements in Sections~\ref{sec:subnetworks} and~\ref{sec:density}.

\subsection{Cached responses, gradient overlap, and gradient norms}
\label{app:gradient_overlap}

These raw-gradient diagnostics use the M-PG/100 student, whereas Figure~\ref{fig:subnetwork_overview}(b,c) measures teacher-conditioned optimizer steps at M-PG/500. Batches use seeds 42 and 43, one response per domain per batch, a 2,048-token prompt cap, and a 256-token generation cap. All eight responses reach the cap. Four response positions are sampled without replacement per response. Each response's four prefixes receive equal weight. For domain-specific inputs, each teacher scores the response from its own domain; for shared inputs, each teacher scores all four responses with equal domain weights. A third control evaluates every teacher on every input domain separately, keeping four prefixes per gradient. These conditions reuse the same responses, allowing paired comparisons within each batch.

Let $S_d(\rho)$ contain the largest-magnitude $\rho$ fraction of teacher $d$'s gradient or update coordinates, with stable coordinate-index tie breaking. Jaccard overlap $J(d,e)=|S_d(\rho)\cap S_e(\rho)|/|S_d(\rho)\cup S_e(\rho)|$ measures shared locations; cosine also captures signs and magnitudes \citep{yu2026denseupdates}. On domain-specific inputs, the first batch's instruction-following PG gradient has norm $6.13\times10^{-9}$. Cosine measures directional opposition, while the gradient norm determines its magnitude. Independent uniform sets of fraction $\rho$ have expected-intersection to expected-union ratio $\rho/(2-\rho)$, approximately 0.005 at $\rho=1\%$.

The largest-magnitude 1\% of raw-gradient coordinates give mean Jaccard overlaps of 0.095 for PG and 0.100 for I64 on domain-specific inputs, averaging the two batches and six teacher pairs. Their ranges are 0.074--0.108 and 0.074--0.118. When every teacher scores all four domain responses, the respective means are 0.38 and 0.46. The teacher-by-input-domain comparison below uses four prefixes per gradient.

Holding the teacher fixed while changing the input domain gives mean gradient cosines of 0.006 for PG and 0.007 for I64. Holding the input domain fixed while changing teachers produces both aligned and opposed gradients. For domain-specific inputs the mean cosines are $-0.002$ and $0.008$, respectively, alongside partial coordinate overlap. Figure~\ref{fig:teacher} shows how input choice affects the teacher-overlap measurements in Section~\ref{sec:subnetworks}.

For I64 (Equation~\ref{eq:topk}), candidate sets and teacher scores are fixed at rollout.
