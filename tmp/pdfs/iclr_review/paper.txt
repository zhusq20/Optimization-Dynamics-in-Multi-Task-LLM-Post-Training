009                                              A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-task LLM post-training has two separate design choices. Task weights spec-
012            ify the capabilities that training should optimize, while task sampling decides
013            where the system spends rollout, reward, and teacher compute. Treating these
014            choices as the same silently changes the multi-task objective whenever the sam-
015            pling distribution changes.
016            We give a task-sampling framework for multi-task reinforcement learning (RL)
017            and multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). With an exact teacher, each KL-
018            regularized RL task is a scaled reverse-KL distillation task, and a simple temper-
019            ature correction aligns their multi-task objectives. For either training mode, we
020
               sample a task using a proposal distribution and multiply its gradient by the target-
               to-proposal ratio. This preserves the intended weighted update while allowing
021
               non-uniform compute allocation.
022
023
               Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) estimates each task’s update
               scale online and samples in proportion to its target weight and estimated scale.
024
               The score can use raw gradients or the scaling already stored by AdamW, requires
025
               no additional model or teacher evaluation, and admits a cost-aware extension. In
026            four-teacher MOPD, we directly audit estimator error, optimizer geometry, and
027            score staleness, then compare against uniform, balanced, and loss-adaptive sam-
028            pling under matched budgets. The confirmatory endpoint is held-out weighted
029            teacher-loss efficiency; downstream benchmarks measure whether the fixed per-
030            task capability trade-off is preserved.

033   1   I NTRODUCTION
034
035   Post-training a language model on mathematics, code, instruction following, and science is a multi-
036   task learning problem. A practical training system must make two decisions. First, it chooses how
037   important the tasks are. Second, it chooses which task supplies the next rollout and gradient. The
038
      first decision defines the target model; the second allocates a finite training budget.
039   These decisions are often coupled. A system may sample a task more frequently because it is
040   difficult, under-trained, or inexpensive, and then apply the sampled gradient without correction. The
041   sampling probabilities consequently become the effective task weights. An apparent optimization
042   improvement may therefore come from solving a different multi-task objective, with a different
043   capability trade-off.
044   This issue occurs in two common forms of LLM post-training. Multi-task reinforcement learning
045   (RL) samples prompts from several reward-bearing tasks and optimizes one shared policy. Multi-
046   teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) instead routes each student rollout to a task teacher and sup-
047   plies dense teacher feedback (Ma et al., 2026a). The supervision differs, but the allocation problem
048   is the same: how should a fixed target task mixture share a limited number of task-homogeneous
049   updates?
050   We connect the two settings through KL-regularized RL. For an exact teacher, the reverse-KL distil-
051   lation objective equals the RL objective up to a task-dependent temperature and an additive constant.
052   The corresponding multi-task objectives agree, up to one global scale, after calibrating the MOPD
053   weights by the task temperatures. This identity motivates a common interface: each task supplies a
      stochastic update, regardless of whether that update was produced by a reward or a teacher.

054
      We then separate target weights from sampling probabilities. If task i has target weight λi but
055   is sampled with probability qi , multiplying its stochastic gradient by λi /qi preserves the intended
056   weighted update. This permits adaptive allocation without changing the target task mixture. The
057   variance-minimizing proposal for a fixed optimizer scaling samples in proportion to the target weight
058   and the task’s stochastic update scale, a standard importance-sampling calculation applied at the
059   task-batch level (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018).
060
      Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) turns this calculation into a short training recipe.
061   It maintains one moving score per task, applies a probability floor, samples one task-homogeneous
062   fixed-token batch, and uses the target-to-proposal correction before the optimizer update. GPAS
063   reuses the training gradient and therefore adds no model, reward, or teacher evaluation. For AdamW,
064   its score uses the optimizer’s lagged coordinate scaling; for SGD, it reduces to the raw gradient root
065   mean square.
066
      The paper makes three contributions:
067
068          • We place multi-task KL-RL and MOPD in one weighted-task framework and give the tem-
069            perature calibration that aligns their exact-teacher vector fields.
070
             • We distinguish the target task mixture from compute allocation and derive one gradient-
071            preserving task sampler for both training modes.
072
             • We provide a practical online algorithm and a theory-aligned MOPD evaluation that di-
073
               rectly audits its estimator, optimizer proxy, online tracking, and training efficiency under a
074
               fixed teacher mixture.
075
076   Section 3 defines the two training modes and their common task-sampling interface. Section 4
077   derives GPAS. Section 5 evaluates the sampler in four-teacher MOPD, and Section 6 gives the im-
078   plementation recipe. The signal–noise and unequal-cost details are deferred to the appendix.

      2   R ELATED W ORK
081
082
      Multi-task learning. Multi-task methods commonly modify objective weights or combine task
083
      gradients to improve balance and reduce interference (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu
084   et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2018). These methods change the target update or the trade-off among tasks.
085   We study a different decision: how to allocate stochastic task batches after the target weights have
086   been chosen. This separation makes the resulting capability trade-off explicit and permits direct
087   comparisons under one fixed multi-task objective.
088
089   Multi-task RL for language models. Joint RL post-training can produce imbalanced gradients
090   and uneven capability progress across tasks (Ramesh et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Adaptive multi-
091   task RL methods often sample weak or slowly improving tasks more frequently (Corrado et al.,
092   2026). Such rules can be useful when the goal is to change task priorities. GPAS instead treats
093   the desired task weights as input and uses importance correction so that adaptive sampling changes
094
      compute allocation without changing those priorities.
095
096
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD trains a shared student from routed task teachers on
      student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026a). Related systems use task-specific teacher interfaces
097
      and language-specialized teachers (Ma et al., 2026b; Xie et al., 2026). Open-MOPD identifies re-
098
      sponse length, teacher–student gaps, and stale signals as causes of capability imbalance (Gao et al.,
099   2026). We use fixed-token task-homogeneous batches to remove length as an implicit task weight
100   and apply the same target-versus-allocation distinction used for multi-task RL.
101
102   Importance sampling for stochastic optimization. Sampling examples in proportion to a gra-
103   dient scale and applying the inverse sampling correction is a classical way to reduce stochastic-
104   gradient variance (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). We apply this idea at the
105   task-batch level, where one draw controls prompt selection, rollout generation, reward or teacher
106   evaluation, and the subsequent optimizer update. The contribution is the unified task-update for-
107   mulation, together with an online optimizer-aware implementation and a direct four-teacher MOPD
      evaluation.

      6   P RACTICAL T RAINING R ECIPE
385
386
      GPAS changes task allocation, not task priorities. The following recipe is the same for multi-task
387
      RL and MOPD.
388
389
      1. Write down the target task weights. Choose RL weights w from the intended capability
390   priorities. For MOPD that is meant to reproduce a KL-RL scalarization, use ρi ∝ wi βi from
391   Equation 5. Do not use the task proposal as a substitute for these weights.
392
393   2. Make a task draw comparable. Use one task-homogeneous batch as the sampling unit and
394   target the same number of valid response tokens across tasks. Normalize the task update before
395   applying its target weight or importance correction. Record actual tokens and wall-clock because
396   long responses and expensive scorers can still change the cost of a draw.
397
398   3. Initialize the online scores. Run a short round-robin warm start so every task has several
399   observations. A practical four-task default is 16 updates, four per task. Maintain an EMA of the
400   squared update score with γ = 0.95. Use raw gradient norm for SGD; for AdamW, use the lagged
401   scaling in Equation 14.
402
403   4. Sample, correct, and update. Form qi ∝ λi sbi with a probability floor. For four tasks, qmin =
404

415   7   D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
416
417   The central multi-task distinction is simple. Target weights determine which capability trade-off the
418   shared model is trained to reach; task sampling determines how a finite compute budget is allocated
419   on the way there. Adaptive sampling without correction mixes these roles. GPAS separates them by
420   pairing a non-uniform task proposal with a target-to-proposal correction.
421   The task-update framework applies to both reward-based and teacher-based post-training. Exact
422   teachers make KL-RL and reverse-KL distillation equivalent after calibrating the MOPD weights by
423   βi . Practical RL and MOPD use different stochastic updates, but the sampling interface does not
424   change: estimate each task’s optimizer-scaled update size, allocate batches accordingly, and preserve
425   the chosen weighted update. The method therefore complements, rather than replaces, approaches
426   that deliberately change task weights to improve a weak capability or a worst-task objective.
427   There are three important limits. First, importance correction preserves the mean of the implemented
428   task update; a biased semi-gradient remains biased. Second, for AdamW it does not make the com-
429   plete optimizer trajectory invariant to task order or proposal. Third, exact-teacher equivalence does
430   not hold for an arbitrary frozen teacher. These limits motivate reporting the full per-task capabil-
431   ity vector, corrected-gradient diagnostics, and optimizer-state checks instead of inferring objective
      preservation from a macro average alone.

435   problem clear. Our experiments establish the empirical claim only for the MOPD setting studied
436   here; validating the same allocation in multi-task RL remains future work.
