009                                              A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-task LLM post-training has two separate design choices. Task weights spec-
012            ify the capabilities that training should optimize, while task sampling decides
013            where the system spends rollout, reward, and teacher compute. Treating these
014            choices as the same silently changes the multi-task objective whenever the sam-
015            pling distribution changes.
016            We give a task-sampling framework for multi-task reinforcement learning (RL)
017            and multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). With an exact teacher, each KL-
018            regularized RL task is a scaled reverse-KL distillation task, and a simple temper-
019            ature correction aligns their multi-task objectives. For either training mode, we
020
               sample a task using a proposal distribution and multiply its gradient by the target-
               to-proposal ratio. This preserves the intended weighted update while allowing
021
               non-uniform compute allocation.
022
023
               Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) estimates each task’s update
               scale online and samples in proportion to its target weight and estimated scale.
024
               The score can use raw gradients or the scaling already stored by AdamW, requires
025
               no additional model or teacher evaluation, and admits a cost-aware extension.
026            We distinguish two practical on-policy distillation updates: sampled-token policy
027            gradient is conditionally unbiased for the full-vocabulary prefix reverse-KL semi-
028            gradient, whereas teacher-top-k distillation preserves a truncated update whose
029            omitted-mass bias we make explicit. We evaluate GPAS in four-teacher MOPD
030            and in three-task GRPO on public verifiable math, code, and science tasks. The
031            studies directly audit estimator error, optimizer geometry, score staleness, fixed-
032            mixture training efficiency, downstream capability, and measured systems cost.

      1   I NTRODUCTION
036
037   Post-training a language model on mathematics, code, instruction following, and science is a multi-
038
      task learning problem. A practical training system must make two decisions. First, it chooses how
      important the tasks are. Second, it chooses which task supplies the next rollout and gradient. The
039
      first decision defines the target model; the second allocates a finite training budget.
040
041   These decisions are often coupled. A system may sample a task more frequently because it is
042   difficult, under-trained, or inexpensive, and then apply the sampled gradient without correction. The
043   sampling probabilities consequently become the effective task weights. An apparent optimization
044
      improvement may therefore come from solving a different multi-task objective, with a different
      capability trade-off.
045
046   This issue occurs in two common forms of LLM post-training. Multi-task reinforcement learning
047   (RL) samples prompts from several reward-bearing tasks and optimizes one shared policy. Multi-
048   teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) instead routes each student rollout to a task teacher and sup-
049   plies dense teacher feedback (Ma et al., 2026a). The supervision differs, but the allocation problem
050
      is the same: how should a fixed target task mixture share a limited number of task-homogeneous
      updates?
051
052   We connect the two settings through KL-regularized RL. For an exact teacher, the reverse-KL distil-
053   lation objective equals the RL objective up to a task-dependent temperature and an additive constant.
      The corresponding multi-task objectives agree, up to one global scale, after calibrating the MOPD

054
      weights by the task temperatures. This identity motivates a common interface: each task supplies a
055   stochastic update, regardless of whether that update was produced by a reward or a teacher.
056
057   We then separate target weights from sampling probabilities. If task i has target weight λi but
058
      is sampled with probability qi , multiplying its stochastic gradient by λi /qi preserves the intended
      weighted update. This permits adaptive allocation without changing the target task mixture. The
059
      variance-minimizing proposal for a fixed optimizer scaling samples in proportion to the target weight
060
      and the task’s stochastic update scale, a standard importance-sampling calculation applied at the
061   task-batch level (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018).
062
063   Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) turns this calculation into a short training recipe.
064
      It maintains one moving score per task, applies a probability floor, samples one task-homogeneous
      fixed-token batch, and uses the target-to-proposal correction before the optimizer update. GPAS
065
      reuses the training gradient and therefore adds no model, reward, or teacher evaluation. For AdamW,
066
      its score uses the optimizer’s lagged coordinate scaling; for SGD, it reduces to the raw gradient root
067   mean square.
068
069   The preserved object is the update actually implemented by the trainer. This distinction is important
070
      for on-policy distillation. A sampled-token policy-gradient (PG) update is an unbiased estimator of
      the full-vocabulary reverse-KL semi-gradient conditional on the sampled prefixes. A teacher-top-k
071
      update instead optimizes a truncated token objective and generally has an omitted-vocabulary bias.
072
      GPAS preserves the conditional mean of either update; it does not turn the top-k implementation
073   into exact sequence-level reverse KL. The same interface also accepts a task-homogeneous PPO or
074   GRPO update.
075
076
      The paper makes three contributions:
077          • We place multi-task KL-RL and MOPD in one weighted-task framework and give the tem-
078            perature calibration that aligns their exact-teacher vector fields.
079          • We distinguish the target task mixture from compute allocation and derive one gradient-
080            preserving task sampler for both training modes.
081
             • We relate the PG and teacher-top-k OPD implementations to their corresponding update
082            fields and identify the approximation introduced by top-k truncation.
083
             • We provide a practical online algorithm and evaluate it under a fixed teacher mixture in
084
               MOPD and a fixed reward mixture in multi-task GRPO, directly auditing estimator error,
085            optimizer proxy, online tracking, training efficiency, and systems cost.
086
087   Section 3 defines the two training modes and their common task-sampling interface. Section 4
088   derives GPAS. Section 5 evaluates the sampler in four-teacher MOPD, and tests transfer to multi-task
089   GRPO. Appendix A gives the implementation recipe; the OPD derivations, signal–noise analysis,
090   and unequal-cost details are also deferred to the appendix.

092   2   R ELATED W ORK
093
094   Multi-task learning. Multi-task methods commonly modify objective weights or combine task
095   gradients to improve balance and reduce interference (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu
096   et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2018). These methods change the target update or the trade-off among tasks.
097   We study a different decision: how to allocate stochastic task batches after the target weights have
098
      been chosen. This separation makes the resulting capability trade-off explicit and permits direct
      comparisons under one fixed multi-task objective.
099
100
      Multi-task RL for language models. Joint RL post-training can produce imbalanced gradients
101
      and uneven capability progress across tasks (Ramesh et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Adaptive multi-
102   task RL methods often sample weak or slowly improving tasks more frequently (Corrado et al.,
103   2026). Such rules can be useful when the goal is to change task priorities. Multi-Task GRPO addi-
104   tionally learns a robustness-oriented task weighting and preserves its realized batch ratios (Ramesh
105   et al., 2026). GPAS instead treats the desired task weights as fixed input and uses importance cor-
106   rection so that adaptive sampling changes compute allocation without changing those priorities. We
107   include Multi-Task GRPO as an objective-changing reference, not as evidence about optimization
      of the same scalarization.


                                                        2
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




108
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD trains a shared student from routed task teachers on
109   student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026a). Related systems use task-specific teacher interfaces
110   and language-specialized teachers (Ma et al., 2026b; Xie et al., 2026). Open-MOPD identifies re-
111   sponse length, teacher–student gaps, and stale signals as causes of capability imbalance (Gao et al.,
112   2026). We use fixed-token task-homogeneous batches to remove length as an implicit task weight,
113   compare with its gap-aware allocation, and apply the same target-versus-allocation distinction used
114   for multi-task RL. We also distinguish the sampled-token PG and teacher-top-k realizations of the
115   OPD update instead of treating either one as the exact sequence-level reverse-KL gradient.
116
117   Importance sampling for stochastic optimization. Sampling examples in proportion to a gra-
118   dient scale and applying the inverse sampling correction is a classical way to reduce stochastic-
119
      gradient variance (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). We apply this idea at the
      task-batch level, where one draw controls prompt selection, rollout generation, reward or teacher
120
      evaluation, and the subsequent optimizer update. PiKE also adapts large-scale multi-task data mix-
121
      tures using task-gradient magnitude, variance, and interaction information (Li et al., 2025). Unlike
122   PiKE and dynamic task-reweighting methods, our proposal is constrained to preserve a separately
123   specified target mixture through λI /qI . The contribution is this fixed-objective formulation together
124   with an online optimizer-aware implementation and direct MOPD and GRPO evaluations.

      5.1   F OUR - TEACHER MOPD SETUP
295
296   We use a frozen revision of Qwen3-1.7B as the common student initialization θ0 (Qwen Team,
297   2025). Four teachers are obtained by separately post-training θ0 on mathematics, code, instruction
298   following, and science question answering, and are frozen before MOPD. We report the model, tok-
299   enizer, and teacher hashes and the complete four-domain capability vector of every teacher. Teacher
300   checkpoints are selected without access to the MOPD results.
301   The primary MOPD implementation is sampled-token PG from Section 3.2, without log-ratio clip-
302   ping. Prefixes and response lengths are stop-gradient quantities, so its target is the response-
303   normalized prefix semi-gradient rather than the exact sequence objective. A prespecified imple-
304   mentation ablation uses teacher-top-k with k = 16 and Equation 8. It applies GPAS to the top-k
305   update itself and does not claim that importance correction removes truncation bias. At the five
306   frozen audit checkpoints, a smaller full-vocabulary prefix bank measures the omitted update norm
307   and its cosine with the top-k update. Three paired seeds repeat Uniform and Adam GPAS under
308
      top-k; the PG implementation remains the confirmatory endpoint.
309   For each task we freeze 16,000 unique training prompts and disjoint sets for score calibration,
310   gradient evaluation, teacher-loss evaluation, and formal benchmarking. Calibration and gradient-
311   evaluation sets use different prompt identities and rollout random-number streams. Dataset revi-
312   sions, licenses, split manifests, de-duplication results, and SHA-256 hashes are released with the
313   experiment record. Thus a proposal is never estimated and evaluated on the same gradient bank.
314   The target mixture is fixed at ρi = 1/4. One optimizer update uses a task-homogeneous batch
315   targeting 65,536 valid response tokens; each response is normalized by its valid-token count before
316   task weighting or importance correction. All runs use 600 updates, a response cap of 8,192 tokens,
317   temperature 1, top-p 1, and thinking disabled. AdamW uses zero weight decay so that the task-
318   dependent step diagnostic below is unambiguous. The learning rate, warmup, and common clipping
319   threshold are chosen once on a uniform-sampling pilot that is excluded from confirmatory analysis.
320   For each training seed, all methods share the first 16 round-robin updates and then branch from
321   the same model, AdamW state, sampler observations, task cursors, and random-number states.
322   These updates count toward every method’s budget and objective-curve area. Separate determin-
323   istic prompt and rollout streams are maintained per task, so the kth visit to a task uses the same data

467   6     D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
468
469   The central multi-task distinction is simple. Target weights determine which capability trade-off the
470   shared model is trained to reach; task sampling determines how a finite compute budget is allocated
471   on the way there. Adaptive sampling without correction mixes these roles. GPAS separates them by
472   pairing a non-uniform task proposal with a target-to-proposal correction.
473
      The task-update framework applies to both reward-based and teacher-based post-training. Exact
474   teachers make KL-RL and sequence-level reverse-KL distillation equivalent after calibrating the
475   MOPD weights by βi . Practical PG, teacher-top-k, and GRPO trainers implement different stochas-
476   tic updates, but the sampling interface does not change: estimate each task’s optimizer-scaled update
477   size, allocate batches accordingly, and preserve the chosen weighted update. The method therefore
478   complements, rather than replaces, approaches that deliberately change task weights to improve a
479   weak capability or a worst-task objective.
480   There are three important limits. First, importance correction preserves the mean of the implemented
481   task update; a biased semi-gradient remains biased. Second, for AdamW it does not make the com-
482   plete optimizer trajectory invariant to task order or proposal. Third, exact-teacher equivalence does
483   not hold for an arbitrary frozen teacher, and teacher-top-k generally omits part of the full-vocabulary
484   update. These limits motivate reporting the full per-task capability vector, corrected-gradient diag-
485   nostics, top-k omitted-mass audits, and optimizer-state checks instead of inferring objective preser-
      vation from a macro average alone.


                                                        9
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




486
      The practical message is that multi-task post-training need not choose between a fixed task mixture
487   and adaptive compute allocation. With explicit target weights and importance correction, one sam-
488   pler can allocate task batches in either multi-task RL or MOPD while keeping the intended learning
489   problem clear. The MOPD study tests the teacher-based mechanism in depth, while the public
490   three-task GRPO study provides a direct reward-based transfer test under a fixed scalarization.

586   A    P RACTICAL T RAINING R ECIPE
587
588   GPAS changes task allocation, not task priorities. The following recipe is the same for multi-task
589   RL and MOPD.
590
591   1. Write down the target task weights. Choose RL weights w from the intended capability
592   priorities. For MOPD that is meant to reproduce a KL-RL scalarization, use ρi ∝ wi βi from
593   Equation 5. Do not use the task proposal as a substitute for these weights. With arbitrary frozen
      teachers, ρ instead defines the desired distillation scalarization directly.


                                                      11
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




594
      2. Make a task draw comparable. Use one task-homogeneous batch as the sampling unit and
595   target the same number of valid response tokens across tasks. Normalize the task update before
596   applying its target weight or importance correction. Record actual tokens and wall-clock because
597   long responses and expensive scorers can still change the cost of a draw. For OPD, record whether
598   Gi is sampled-token PG or teacher-top-k; for GRPO, keep the within-policy ratio separate from the
599   task-level target-to-proposal factor.
600
601   3. Initialize the online scores. Run a short round-robin warm start so every task has several
602   observations. A practical four-task default is 16 updates, four per task. Maintain an EMA of the
603   squared update score with γ = 0.95. Use raw gradient norm for SGD; for AdamW, use the lagged
604
      scaling in Equation 16.
605
      4. Sample, correct, and update. Form qi ∝ λi sbi with a probability floor. For four tasks,
606
      qmin = 0.05 is a conservative starting value. Draw one task, compute its normalized update, ap-
607
      ply any fixed per-task clipping rule, multiply by λI /qI , and then update the optimizer. Applying
608   nonlinear clipping after the importance multiplier generally breaks exact mean preservation. The
609   correction preserves the chosen PG, top-k, or GRPO update field; it does not remove approximation
610   bias already present inside that field.
611
612   5. Save and audit the sampler. Checkpoint the EMA values, timestamps, proposal, task cursors,
613   and random state. Log I, qI , λI , λI /qI , valid tokens, and the raw and optimizer-scaled gradient
614   norms. Inspect the maximum importance ratio and the age of every task score. A task that repeatedly
615   hits the floor needs either a higher floor or more frequent score refreshes.
