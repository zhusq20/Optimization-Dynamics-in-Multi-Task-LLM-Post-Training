011                                              A BSTRACT
012
013            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) aims to consolidate specialized ca-
014            pabilities in a single student. We examine three aspects of its optimization: to-
015            ken balancing, update sparsity, and supervision density. Starting from the MOPD
016            learning objective, we first show how loss averaging determines both domain
017            weights and the relative weight of long and short responses. A finite-batch gradi-
018            ent decomposition separates these effects and motivates matched experiments on
019
               capability balance. Second, we investigate whether parameter changes concen-
               trate on small, overlapping subsets when different teachers act on the same MOPD
020
               student, and whether more different teacher distributions correspond to more dif-
021
               ferent subsets. We measure optimizer steps and cumulative parameter changes
022            separately, using raw gradients to help explain the observed updates. Third, we
023            compare sampled-token policy gradients with teacher top-k supervision in single-
024            teacher and joint training, using full-vocabulary reverse-KL updates at shared pre-
025            fixes as a local reference. The planned experiments pair update concentration with
026            per-domain capability and account for sampling variability, training exposure, and
027            compute. Each question is developed together with its experimental design, with-
028            out presuming that sparse updates, low teacher overlap, or sampled supervision
029            improve capability integration. Empirical outcomes remain to be measured.

032   1   I NTRODUCTION
033
034
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) transfers domain specialists into one student by scoring
035   student-generated responses with their assigned teachers (Ma et al., 2026). The objective is to inte-
036   grate their capabilities, but equal numbers of prompts from each domain need not give those domains
037   equal influence on training. Open-MOPD identifies capability imbalance and studies token-share
038   weighting, changing distillation gaps, and stale student-dependent rewards (Gao et al., 2026). These
039   findings motivate examining how the objective weights each domain and which student parameters
040   its teacher changes most.
041   Prior work reports sparse cumulative parameter changes in RL (Mukherjee et al., 2025) and OPD
042   (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). We use update sparsity to describe concentration of actual
043   parameter changes, distinguishing individual optimizer steps from cumulative changes over training.
044   We ask how multiple teachers affect a common student during MOPD, and use gradients as an
045   additional measurement to separate the current loss signal from optimizer history. Independently
046   trained students provide a reference, but their endpoints do not identify teacher contributions within
047   the joint student.
048   After introducing the MOPD learning objective in Section 2, we develop three studies and place
049   each experiment alongside its corresponding question:
050
051         1. Token balancing (Section 3). We derive how global-token, domain-token, and domain-
052            response averaging weight domains and response lengths, then compare their effects on
053            capability balance. The analysis explains existing loss reductions without introducing a
               new allocation algorithm.

      4     U PDATE S PARSITY AND T EACHER OVERLAP
169
170
      We ask whether OPD parameter updates concentrate on a small fraction of parameters, whether
171   different teachers affect the same parameters within one MOPD student, and whether teacher distri-
172   bution differences predict this overlap. Each question includes its planned experiment; no outcome
173   is assumed.
174
175   4.1   D OES UPDATE SPARSITY PERSIST UNDER MULTIPLE TEACHERS ?
176
177   Prior work reports concentrated parameter changes in RL fine-tuning (Mukherjee et al., 2025) and
178   OPD (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). We measure cumulative parameter changes ∆ = θ −θ0 and
179   actual optimizer steps u = θafter − θbefore separately. For each, we report the fraction of parameters
180
      with absolute change at most a threshold τ , together with the fraction of squared norm carried by
      the largest-magnitude ρ fraction of parameters. We sweep τ and ρ: “update sparsity” here means
181
      near-zero parameter changes or concentrated changes, not necessarily exact zeros. Per-step and
182
      cumulative sparsity can differ because successive updates can cancel.
183
184   Experiment. Using the representative single-teacher and joint runs from Section 5, record cumu-
185   lative changes at early, intermediate, and final checkpoints and actual optimizer steps around those
186   checkpoints and initialization. Report curves against both domain exposure and compute. Mea-
187   sure differences using FP32 master weights when available and report exported BF16 checkpoint
188   differences separately to identify rounding-induced zeros; converting BF16 values to FP32 cannot
189   recover lost changes. Threshold curves, norms, and energy concentration jointly show whether ap-
190   parent sparsity reflects small overall scale or concentration on particular parameters.
191   As a secondary diagnostic, compare each domain’s raw gradient gd = ∇θ Ld and the combined
192   MOPD gradient under the same loss reduction, where Ld is that domain’s mean loss. Match diagnos-
193   tic response counts per domain across single-teacher and joint students, report valid-token counts,
194   and check sensitivity to batch size; record the combined gradient’s full batch size separately. Repeat
195   over fresh student-rollout batches, record accumulation denominators, and accumulate gradients in
196   FP32 before clipping or optimizer processing. Apply the same threshold, norm, and concentration
197   summaries to gradients, keeping their scales separate from steps and cumulative changes. AdamW
198
      momentum and weight decay can move parameters with a zero current gradient, so gradient sparsity
      alone cannot establish update sparsity.

248   5     D OES S UPERVISION D ENSITY C HANGE U PDATE S PARSITY ?
249
250   Our third study asks whether vocabulary-level supervision changes how strongly parameter updates
251   concentrate on a small fraction of model parameters. Sampled-token PG uses feedback for the
252
      generated token, full-vocabulary reverse KL includes every vocabulary alternative, and teacher top-
      k provides a practical intermediate choice (Section 2). This concerns vocabulary coverage at each
253
      prefix, rather than the number of supervised response positions. Sampled-token feedback does not
254
      imply a sparse parameter gradient: the sampled token’s log probability depends on all logits through
255   the softmax normalizer. Concentrated OPD parameter changes have also been observed with dense
256   feedback (Yu et al., 2026). We therefore test for a difference without assuming that PG produces
257   sparser updates or better capability integration.

285
286
      Our primary measurements are actual online steps and cumulative parameter changes from initial-
287
      ization, reported separately. Following Section 4, we report threshold curves, norms, and the fraction

      5.3   I NTERPRETATION AND DECISION RULE
301
302   At a fixed prefix, fresh unclipped sampled-token PG with a detached log-ratio coefficient has the
303   same expected gradient as full-vocabulary reverse KL (Yu et al., 2026; Oh et al., 2026). This identity
304   holds with fixed prefix weights; weighting an original rollout token by its eventual response length
305   need not preserve it. Clipping and top-k truncation also change the comparison, and equality in
306   expectation does not equate sampled gradients, nonlinear optimizer steps, or training trajectories.
307   We report paired-seed effect estimates and confidence intervals against a material difference margin
308   specified before examining results. One-seed runs remain exploratory; prompt resampling measures
309   evaluation uncertainty, not training-seed variation. Intervals entirely within the margin support no
310   material sparsity difference in the tested regime; intervals spanning negligible and material effects
311   are inconclusive. Only a stable, meaningful gap motivates the optional sampling-variance and trun-
312   cation controls in Appendix C.3.

319   Capability integration and task weighting. MOPD consolidates routed domain teachers in pol-
320   icy space (Ma et al., 2026). Open-MOPD diagnoses token-share and reward-related imbalances
321   (Gao et al., 2026). Our first study separates cross-domain weighting from within-domain response-
322   length weighting in existing loss reductions. Prior work on multi-task loss weighting and gradient
323   conflict also motivates distinguishing weight balance from joint capability gains (Chen et al., 2018;
      Yu et al., 2020).

      Sparse parameter changes and teacher differences. Mukherjee et al. (2025) report sparse cu-
325   mulative parameter changes in RL and repeated changes in similar parameter subsets across settings.
326   Yu et al. (2026) establish related OPD signatures, including overlap when varying teachers and ex-
327   periments that restrict training to selected parameters. Shen et al. (2026) examine update subspaces
328   and response-position thinning. These results motivate our study but do not establish sparsity of
329   instantaneous gradients. Teacher compatibility also affects distillation success (Li et al., 2026; Ma
330   et al., 2026). We measure single-step and cumulative parameter changes separately and ask whether
331   teachers with more different distributions change more different subsets of a shared MOPD student.
332   Raw gradients help explain the role of the current loss signal.
333
334   Vocabulary coverage and gradient estimation. MOPD provides sampled-token and corrected
335   teacher-top-k objectives (Ma et al., 2026). Variance-reduced sampled OPD (Oh et al., 2026), exact-
336   head plus sampled-tail estimation (Zhang & Ba, 2026), and tail-aware losses (Huang & Wei, 2026)
337   motivate separating vocabulary coverage from the choice of objective and estimator. Our loss study
338   compares update concentration under practical PG and top-k training, with full-vocabulary local
339   updates and their gradients in both single-teacher and joint settings. Established estimator controls
340   are reserved for interpreting a stable difference; we do not propose a new estimator.

      7   D ISCUSSION
344
345   The three studies connect loss weighting, parameter-update concentration in a shared MOPD stu-
346   dent, and vocabulary-level supervision. Their interpretation depends on distinguishing raw gradients
347   from optimizer steps and cumulative parameter changes. A concentrated cumulative change need
348
      not imply that each gradient or optimizer step was similarly concentrated. Overlapping update loca-
      tions can also contain either shared or opposing corrections.
349
350   The PG/top-k comparison tests for a material difference rather than assuming an advantage for sam-
351   pled supervision. A resolved absence of such a difference is informative within the tested regime;
352   a stable gap can motivate further analysis. Likewise, teacher distribution differences may or may
353   not predict which parameters change most. Capability measurements determine whether these opti-
354
      mization patterns accompany useful integration.
