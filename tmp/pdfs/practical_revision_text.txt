009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) combines task-specific teacher feed-
012            back in a shared student. Its training budget must accommodate tasks whose
013            responses differ in length and whose gradients vary in reliability. Dense top-
014            k supervision already uses several teacher scores at each position, but different
015            prompts and student-generated trajectories still produce different updates. We in-
016
               troduce Gradient-Preconditioned Adaptive Sampling (GPAS), a micro-batch allo-
               cation method that uses this remaining variation to decide how much computation
017
               each task receives. GPAS averages each task’s gradients under fixed objective
018
               weights and assigns more micro-batches to tasks with larger fluctuations after the
019            optimizer’s coordinate scaling. It reuses gradients from ordinary training and sup-
020            ports a cost-aware mode that also accounts for task-dependent running time. We
021            study four-domain distillation into Qwen3-1.7B, comparing training efficiency,
022            capability integration, and allocation dynamics under existing dense distillation
023            losses. [TODO: Report the measured efficiency difference, per-domain capa-

      1   I NTRODUCTION
027
028
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) provides a practical way to combine capabilities in
029
      one language model. The student generates responses, and each response is scored by the teacher
030   assigned to its task (Ma et al., 2026). This allows specialist teachers to be developed separately and
031   their feedback to be combined during student training. The same approach can add a new capability
032   while using an earlier model as a teacher to preserve existing ones (Liu et al., 2026). Once the
033   teachers are available, a recurring engineering question is how to divide the student’s training budget
034   among their tasks.
035
      Existing recipes address several parts of this problem. Open-MOPD adjusts loss weights to com-
036   pensate for response-length imbalance and to emphasize domains with larger teacher–student gaps
037   (Gao et al., 2026). D3 -MOPD changes the sampling mixture using the remaining gap and its recent
038   rate of decrease (Sun et al., 2026). These approaches make the allocation responsive to task balance
039   and learning progress. They also motivate a complementary question: for a chosen balance of task
040   objectives, how many samples are needed to obtain a reliable update from each task?
041
      Consider two equally weighted tasks. One gives similar gradients across micro-batches, while the
042   other gives gradients that fluctuate widely with the sampled prompts and responses. Equal counts
043   spend the same computation on both estimates even though the second is less precise. Giving the
044   second task more micro-batches can make the combined update more reliable. Its objective weight
045   can stay the same: first average the gradients within each task, then combine those averages with
046   the chosen weights. Task importance and the amount of computation used to estimate its update are
047   separate choices.
048   This distinction remains useful with dense distillation. MOPD already supports a teacher top-k loss,
049   and recent methods improve how retained tokens and the distribution tail contribute to supervision
050   (Ma et al., 2026; Huang & Wei, 2026). Even when all retained-token contributions are computed
051   at a fixed prefix, different prompts and student rollouts still yield different gradients. The relevant
052   signal for allocation is therefore the variability of the actual training gradient. Teacher loss alone
053   does not describe that variability: two tasks can have similar losses but different gradient directions
      and response lengths.

075   We propose Gradient-Preconditioned Adaptive Sampling (GPAS), which allocates micro-batches
076   using the gradient variation observed during ordinary MOPD training. GPAS measures this varia-
077   tion after the optimizer’s coordinate scaling. This matters because a fluctuation has a larger effect on
078   an update when the optimizer amplifies the coordinates in which it occurs. With fixed task weights,
079   the resulting rule gives each task a count proportional to its weighted preconditioned standard devi-
080   ation. A cost-aware mode also uses measured running time, allowing the same scheduler to target
081   efficiency per step or per GPU hour.
082   Our study follows this practical sequence: compare learning curves and per-domain capability, ex-
083   amine where GPAS spends computation, and measure the contributions of optimizer scaling and
084   running time. All core allocation comparisons use the same existing top-k loss. A second dense

357   6     R ELATED W ORK
358
359   Multi-teacher distillation in practice. MOPD combines domain teachers through supervision on
360   student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026); recent systems also use a specialist together with
361   an earlier checkpoint to add skills while preserving general capability (Liu et al., 2026). Open-
362   MOPD identifies response-token imbalance, uneven learning progress, and stale student-dependent
363
      rewards, addressing them through loss weighting and reward refresh (Gao et al., 2026). D3 -MOPD
      adapts domain sampling using the remaining teacher gap and its descent velocity (Sun et al., 2026).
364
      GPAS addresses the precision of task-gradient estimates under specified objective weights, using
365
      their measured variability and computational cost.
366
367
      Dense losses and token estimators. MOPD includes both sampled-token policy gradients and
368
      a corrected reverse-KL loss on the teacher’s top-k tokens (Ma et al., 2026). Open-MOPD uses
369   student top-k candidates with probability-weighted log-ratio advantages; its released dense imple-
370   mentation computes candidate-wise losses before summing across the retained set (Gao et al., 2026;
371   BytedTsinghua-SIA, 2026). EMA-PG combines an exact retained-set contribution with a sampled
372   tail (Zhang & Ba, 2026), TA-OPD includes the probability mass outside the teacher’s top-k set
373   (Huang & Wei, 2026), and vOPD uses a control-variate baseline for sampled-token distillation (Oh
374   et al., 2026). We build on existing dense losses and apply GPAS to the micro-batch gradients they
375   produce.
376
377   Learning dynamics and capability integration. Teacher–student compatibility and the capabili-
      ties supplied by the teacher shape OPD outcomes (Li et al., 2026). CaMOPD studies counteracting

      recovery and preservation updates and combines alternating training with gap-based selection (Chen
379   et al., 2026). Recent work also finds that diverse queries can provide broad state coverage while the
380   student continues to absorb supervision slowly (Fu et al., 2026). These studies motivate examin-
381   ing the update process alongside teacher loss. Our measurements relate gradient variability, sample
382   allocation, and learning efficiency.
383
384   Task weighting and stochastic optimization. Multi-task learning adapts loss weights and gradi-
385   ent directions (Chen et al., 2018; Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020); language-model training
386   also adapts data mixtures from learning progress (Albalak et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Impor-
387   tance sampling and online variance reduction improve stochastic gradient estimates (Katharopoulos
388   & Fleuret, 2018; Borsos et al., 2018). GPAS uses within-task standard deviations, following Neyman
389   allocation and its unequal-cost extension (Neyman, 1934; Cochran, 1977). The interaction between
390
      non-uniform sampling and adaptive optimizers (Lahire, 2023) motivates measuring those deviations
      after the optimizer’s coordinate scaling.

393   7   D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
394
395   GPAS treats task importance and gradient-estimation effort as separate decisions. Existing dense
396   distillation provides the learning signal; the scheduler uses its observed variability to decide where
397   more micro-batches are useful. The same principle gives a per-step rule and a cost-aware rule,
398   both using statistics collected during ordinary training. [TODO: Conclude with the measured
399   efficiency difference and the task-level learning pattern that explains it.]
400   The present study covers a Qwen3-1.7B student, four domains, one training seed per method, and
401   one hardware layout. The method is most directly applicable when an optimizer step contains sev-
402   eral micro-batches from each task; larger task collections call for less frequent task measurements.
403   The pre-step optimizer scaling and measured time model provide local allocation signals, and their
404   practical effect is evaluated with the complete AdamW training loop. Task compatibility and student
405   capacity remain important for capability integration. GPAS offers a way to improve how a chosen
406   collection of task objectives uses computation.
