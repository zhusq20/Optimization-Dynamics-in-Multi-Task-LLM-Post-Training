035   1   I NTRODUCTION
036
037   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) merges several specialist teachers into one student: the
038   student generates a response, and the specialist for its task scores that response token by token (Ma
039   et al., 2026). Existing recipes must also choose a task mixture. Token-share balancing changes that
040   mixture to compensate for response-length imbalance (Gao et al., 2026), whereas gap-following
041   changes it as teachers become easier or harder to imitate (Sun et al., 2026). In either case, the same
042   mixture controls what the student learns and how much evidence estimates each task gradient.
043   A mixture is one knob doing two jobs. Sampling a task more often changes both the objective the
044   student minimizes and how many samples estimate that task’s gradient, so any mixture that adapts
045   to training progress changes what is learned and how precisely at the same time. The precision side
046   is not a detail: the noise of a task’s gradient differs across tasks, drifts during training, and reaches
047   the optimizer only after it preconditions each coordinate, so the task whose noise dominates a step
048   depends on which optimizer is used.
049   On-policy distillation weights every sampled token by the difference between the student’s and the
050   teacher’s log-probability. Averaged under the student, this difference is the local reverse-KL gap;
051   after centering, its variation across tokens interacts with the score function to determine the gradient.
052   At one position, two tokens of similar student probability can carry weights of opposite sign and
053   very different magnitude. That spread creates sampling noise and can differ across teachers because
      teachers differ in how far they are from the student. The teacher already computes a distribution at

320   Held-out teacher loss. Held-out teacher loss is the fixed-weight teacher loss on prompts that the
321   training stream never sees. It is the primary training metric and is evaluated with a common sampling
322   seed; method differences use paired bootstrap intervals over prompts, which measure evaluation
323   uncertainty rather than variation over training seeds. Uniform’s final held-out loss is the common

      Gap and token diagnostics. At every step and for every task, we log three scalars from the top-k
325   scores. The gap level is the student-probability-weighted mean log ratio within the top-k set; the
326   within-position spread is the corresponding weighted variance of that log ratio; and the tail mass
327   is one minus the summed student probability of the set. The spread is a low-cost diagnostic rather
328   than the gradient variance itself, because it omits the score vectors’ directions and norms; the paired
329   gradient measurement below tests whether it predicts token-level noise.

335   estimates task noise and chooses an allocation, while the other scores that allocation using V (m);

337   are fixed, the sampled-token and top-k diagnostic gradients share an independent scoring-token draw
338   at every prefix. Their paired variance change isolates the fixed-prefix token mechanism but need not
339   equal the online training-noise change (details in Appendix A.7).

453   6      R ELATED W ORK
454
455   Task weighting and data allocation. Multi-task methods tune loss weights, alter gradient direc-
456   tions, or adapt data schedules (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al.,
457   2018), and language-model training adapts domain, rollout, or prompt distributions from learning
458   progress (Albalak et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). These
459
      methods change the objective or the data distribution. GPAS keeps both fixed and changes only the
      number of micro-batches used to estimate each task gradient; because every task appears in every
460
      step, it observes within-task gradient noise for all tasks rather than for a sampled subset.
461
462
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD routes student rollouts to task teachers and trains
463
      a shared student with token-level teacher feedback (Ma et al., 2026). Open-MOPD studies token-
464   share balancing and gap-following, and also handles stale rewards (Gao et al., 2026); its token-share
465   balance holds here by construction through response averaging and wi /mi weighting. We use one
466   update per rollout and do not address reuse. D3 -MOPD adapts the domain mixture from the teacher-
467   loss gap and its descent velocity (Sun et al., 2026), which changes the objective weights; GPAS
468   changes the precision of each task-gradient estimate instead. Teacher mixtures can still produce
469   transfer and task trade-offs (Li et al., 2026), which is why we report capability per task.
470
471   Top-k distillation estimators. Three estimators are commonly described as top-k distillation. The
472   first restricts reverse KL to the student’s top-k tokens and renormalizes; it drops the mass outside
473   the set and therefore optimizes a different objective, and MOPD reports that it diverges earlier than
474
      the sampled-token form under a large teacher–student gap (Ma et al., 2026). The second, used by
      Open-MOPD, sums top-k log ratios into a scalar reward that multiplies the sampled token’s score
475
      function. This reward is smoother than a single log ratio, but randomness from the sampled token
476
      remains. The third is the sampled-token log ratio itself. Our estimator computes that third form’s
477   top-k contribution exactly and lets a sampled token cover the remaining mass, preserving its fixed-
478   prefix expectation while removing the retained-set sampling component.
479
480   Stratified allocation for optimization. Gradient-norm sampling reduces the second moment of
481   an SGD estimate (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). Neyman allocation assigns
482   samples in proportion to within-stratum standard deviation, and unequal sampling costs give the
483   square-root cost correction used here (Neyman, 1934; Cochran, 1977; Mohri et al., 2026). The right
484   variance geometry depends on the optimizer: Euclidean coordinates suit SGD, whereas adaptive
485   optimizers precondition coordinates (Lahire, 2023). GPAS applies Neyman allocation to fixed-size
      MOPD micro-batches under the preconditioner of whichever optimizer is used.

      7   L IMITATIONS AND C ONCLUSION
487
488
      The study covers one student scale, four tasks, one seed per method, and one system layout. GPAS
489   is a local rule: its analysis treats micro-batches within a step as conditionally independent and
490   identically distributed within task, holds the pre-step preconditioner fixed while measuring noise,
491   and its attainable variance reduction is bounded by the count limits. The additive step-time model is
492   fitted to one layout; systems with parallel critical paths require their own measured time model. The
493   method needs at least two micro-batches per task per step, so settings with many more tasks than
494   micro-batches need a task-subsampling variant. The variance derivation treats fresh micro-batches
495   as conditionally i.i.d.; the fixed shuffled prompt stream is read without replacement and therefore has
496   a finite-population covariance correction. Held-out splits and online estimates measure the realized
497
      setting directly.
498   Each rollout batch is used for one update; staleness caused by reusing rollouts across updates is
499   outside our scope. The top-k estimator also requires the teacher to score the student’s retained
500   tokens and the sampled token when it lies outside that set. This is natural when the teacher is
501   available as a prefill scoring service, but it may not be available through an API that exposes only
502   the sampled-token score.
503   GPAS fixes the objective weights and adapts only how many micro-batches each task contributes,
504   so the allocation changes gradient variance and nothing else. Its per-step mode follows Neyman
505   allocation in preconditioned coordinates, and its cost-aware mode also uses measured time; both
506   reuse statistics produced by MOPD training. [TODO: Conclude with the four empirical laws
