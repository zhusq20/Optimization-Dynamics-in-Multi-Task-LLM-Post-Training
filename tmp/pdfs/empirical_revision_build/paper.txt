009                                              A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation transfers domain expertise into a shared stu-
012            dent. We study how loss averaging, optimizer history, and vocabulary super-
013            vision shape this transfer, using Qwen3-1.7B and four domain specialists, with
014            additional SmolLM3-3B measurements. Token and response averaging produce
015            different gradients whose update directions become more similar after applying
016            saved Adam moments; final task scores are close under top-64 supervision. Train-
017            ing changes about 97% of FP32 parameters, with only 7–9% remaining visibly
018
               changed after BF16 rounding. Adam’s accumulated moments also account for
               much of the alignment between teacher-specific updates: subtracting the zero-
019
               gradient step reduces their mean cosine from above 0.83 to below 0.01 on domain-
020
               specific inputs. Expanding supervision from a sampled token to the top-64 inter-
021            section improves final mathematics accuracy and lowers science accuracy, with
022            relative gains changing during training. Top-64 closely approximates the full-
023            vocabulary gradient direction in the high-coverage Qwen measurements; SmolLM
024            measurements with lower coverage retain appreciable gradient error. Together,
025            these findings connect local update measurements to task-dependent learning tra-
026            jectories and identify optimizer history and numerical precision as central to in-
027            terpreting distillation updates.

030   1   I NTRODUCTION
031
032   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) transfers specialized capabilities into a single model
033
      (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026; NVIDIA, 2026; Kimi Team et al., 2026; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026).
      The student generates responses, and a teacher assigned to each prompt domain supplies token-level
034
      supervision. Improving several capabilities requires deciding how much each domain and response
035
      contributes to the loss, and how many next-token alternatives the teacher supervises.
036
037   Recent work establishes both the potential of MOPD and its sensitivity to these choices. Ma et al.
038   (2026) study capability integration with policy-gradient and top-k objectives. Open-MOPD ad-
039
      dresses uneven transfer through domain token balancing, performance-gap weighting, and refreshed
      rewards (Gao et al., 2026). The Nemotron 3 Ultra report finds no advantage for top-k or full-
040
      vocabulary matching over sampled-token supervision in preliminary experiments (NVIDIA, 2026,
041
      Section 3.3.5). Other studies report parameter changes concentrated in a small set of stored weights
042   (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). How these observations connect loss construction, optimizer
043   updates, and task performance remains open.
044
045
      Our study follows the path from loss construction to gradients, parameter updates, and task per-
      formance. The main experiments train Qwen3-1.7B with teachers for mathematics, code, instruc-
046
      tion following, and science. We vary loss averaging and vocabulary supervision during training,
047
      and compare gradients and optimizer steps at fixed student parameters and inputs. Three findings
048   emerge:
049
050        1. Adam brings different normalization gradients closer in update direction (Section 3).
051           Weighting responses by length or equally produces gradients with mean cosine 0.68; apply-
052           ing the same saved Adam state raises the update cosine to 0.96. Under top-64 supervision,
053           the averaging rules reach similar final mean scores and follow different task-level learning
              curves.


                                                       1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




054
            2. Precision and optimizer history strongly shape measured update sparsity and align-
055            ment (Section 4). Training changes most FP32 weights, while BF16 rounding leaves a
056            small fraction visibly changed. Teacher-specific steps are strongly aligned through shared
057            Adam moments; subtracting the zero-gradient step leaves near-zero mean alignment on
058            domain-specific inputs.
059         3. Broader vocabulary supervision produces task-dependent gains (Section 5). Top-64
060            supervision improves final mathematics accuracy and lowers science accuracy relative to
061            sampled-token supervision. Its mean advantage narrows as training proceeds. At fixed
062            student and optimizer state, the losses produce similar update concentration.
063
064
      Additional SmolLM3-3B measurements show that top-64 retains high average student probability
      while omitting substantial mass on some inputs. Enlarging the candidate set improves gradient
065
      approximation, with residual error relative to full-vocabulary supervision. Section 6 connects these
066
      observations to checkpoint selection: individual tasks can regress during training and recover by
067   the endpoint, and development-set rankings can change on separate test questions. These results
068   motivate tracking per-task learning curves alongside gradients and parameter changes.

      3.1   R ESPONSE LENGTH CHANGES THE CONTRIBUTION OF EACH DOMAIN AND RESPONSE
116
      Longer responses receive more weight when the loss is averaged over all tokens. In our training
117   batches, mathematics accounts for about three times as many response tokens as instruction fol-
118   lowing, even though the two domains receive equal numbers of prompts (Figure 1a). Global token
119   averaging therefore gives mathematics a larger share of the loss. Averaging each domain separately
120   fixes its total contribution; averaging response losses equally also removes the extra weight assigned
121   to long responses.
122
      The remaining effect of length within a domain has a simple form. For response gradients gi = ∇θ ℓi
123   and domain means ḡd , T̄d , the token-weighted gradient is

      3.2   T RAINED A DAM MOMENTS ALIGN UPDATES FROM DIFFERENT AVERAGING RULES
133
134   Different gradients produce closely aligned Adam steps. On identical student responses, equal
135   response weighting and within-domain token weighting produce gradients with mean cosine 0.68.
136   Applying those gradients to copies of the same saved Adam state raises their update cosine to 0.96
137   (Figure 1b). Comparisons with global token averaging show the same pattern. Thus, the optimizer
138   substantially reduces the directional differences introduced by loss averaging.
139   Adam’s response to gradient scaling provides relevant context: its adaptive update is invariant to a
140   fixed positive rescaling of each coordinate throughout the gradient history when ϵ = 0, and approx-
141   imately so when ϵ is negligible (Kingma & Ba, 2015). Domain reweighting also changes gradient
142   direction, and the fixed-state comparisons above measure Adam’s response to those changes directly.
143
      The SmolLM comparisons with zero-initialized Adam moments give lower pairwise cosines than
144
      the raw gradients (Appendix H.3). Together, these observations make the optimizer state central to
145   interpreting update alignment.
146
147   Clipping preserves gradient direction; optimizer history shapes the step. Equal response
148   weighting triggers clipping throughout training, and token weighting triggers it less often (Fig-
149   ure 2a). Global norm clipping preserves pairwise gradient cosines. Resetting Adam’s moments
150   changes step norms and held-out loss changes, while norm-matched SGD steps separate the aver-
151   aging rules more strongly (panels b,c). These interventions show how accumulated moments shape
152   the effect of the current gradient.
153   One-step improvements also vary by domain. At a later checkpoint, token-weighted Adam steps
154   lower average held-out KL while increasing instruction-following KL. The aggregate improvement
155   therefore coexists with a domain-level regression (Appendix G.6).

      4     PARAMETER -U PDATE S PARSITY AND T EACHER A LIGNMENT
223
224
      Two properties of the training system strongly affect how parameter changes appear: the precision
225   used to store weights and the optimizer state shared across updates. We examine both when inter-
226   preting concentrated checkpoint changes (Yu et al., 2026) and alignment between teacher-specific
227   steps.
228
229   4.1   BF16 ROUNDING HIDES WIDESPREAD FP32 CHANGES
230
231   Joint training changes about 97% of FP32 master weights. After BF16 rounding, only 7–9% of
232   weights differ visibly from initialization under sampled-token or top-64 supervision with equal
233   response weighting (Figure 3a). Small updates are widespread in FP32 and disappear when the
234
      weights are stored in BF16.
235   The precision effect also extends to the concentration of update magnitude. About one third of FP32
236   coordinates carry 90% of squared cumulative change, compared with about 4% of BF16 coordinates.
237   We compute these fractions from unique model coordinates and measure nonzero changes relative
238   to initialization; Appendix C gives the definitions and threshold analysis.
239   Loss averaging provides a further comparison between parameter changes and performance. Under
240   top-64 supervision, the averaging rules produce different fractions of changed BF16 weights while
241   reaching similar final mean scores. The changed fraction describes the stored displacement, and its
242   relationship to task performance depends on the training setting.
243
244   4.2   S HARED A DAM MOMENTS ALIGN TEACHER - SPECIFIC UPDATES
245
246   Teacher-specific steps are strongly aligned when they start from the same trained student and saved
247
      Adam state. On responses from each teacher’s own domain, mean update cosines exceed 0.83 under
      both sampled-token and top-64 supervision. Resetting the first moment while retaining the second,
248
      or replacing Adam with norm-matched SGD, reduces the mean cosine to near zero (Figure 3b). The
249
      accumulated first moment accounts for much of the alignment.
250
251   Adam’s first moment can move parameters even with zero current gradient. To measure the change
252   in a step induced by the current teacher gradient, we subtract this baseline. For update function
253
      U (g; H) and saved state H,
                                          δUd = U (gd ; H) − U (0; H).                                (6)
254
255
      Both steps start from identical parameters and state, so δUd measures the current gradient’s contri-
      bution conditional on the saved moments.
256
257   After this subtraction, the mean cosine between teacher-specific step differences falls below 0.01
258   on domain-specific inputs. When all teachers score the same prefixes, the residual cosine is larger,
259   about 0.24–0.35 (Figure 3c). Teacher-specific alignment therefore depends on both shared optimizer
260   history and shared inputs: removing the zero-gradient component reveals much greater directional
261
      separation across domains.

      7   R ELATED W ORK
440
441
      Distillation objectives and vocabulary truncation. MiniLLM uses reverse KL (Gu et al., 2024);
442   Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD) combines student-generated sequences with alterna-
443   tive divergences (Agarwal et al., 2024); f -DISTILL derives word-level losses from sequence-level
444   f -divergences (Wen et al., 2023). Sparse Logit Sampling analyzes top-k caching bias (Anshu-
445   mann et al., 2025), while Tail-Aware OPD restores tail-mass information lost by top-k renormal-
446   ization (Huang & Wei, 2026). Our intersection loss retains full-softmax log ratios with detached
447   student-top-k weights. Section 5 compares its coverage, gradient error, updates, and task scores
448   with sampled-token and full-vocabulary supervision.
449
450   Multi-teacher distillation and loss normalization. MOPD integrates specialized teachers on
451   student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026); Open-MOPD adds domain token balancing and adap-
452
      tive weighting (Gao et al., 2026). DAPO uses token-level averaging (Yu et al., 2025), and Dr. GRPO
      analyzes length and reward-normalization bias (Liu et al., 2025). We separate domain weighting
453
      from within-domain response weighting: Open-MOPD’s token-share correction gives DT for fixed
454
      batches and domain weights, while DR also removes response-length weighting.
455
456   Post-training parameter geometry. Mukherjee et al. (2025) study small parameter subsets sup-
457   porting RL fine-tuning; Yu et al. (2026) report concentrated distillation updates and overlap between
458   separately trained students. Shen et al. (2026) examine OPD geometry and response-position se-
459   lection. We compare teachers at the same student and Adam state, distinguish FP32 from BF16
460   changes, and vary vocabulary support at fixed prefixes.

      8   D ISCUSSION
463
464
      The path from a distillation loss to task performance passes through optimizer history, numeri-
465   cal precision, and an evolving training trajectory. In our comparisons, Adam aligns directionally
466   different gradients, and BF16 rounding hides widespread FP32 changes. Broader vocabulary su-
467   pervision improves gradient approximation and produces task-dependent learning gains, including
468   a mathematics–science trade-off and changing advantages over time. These findings give comple-
469   mentary roles to the measurements: gradients describe the immediate training signal, parameter
470   changes show its interaction with the optimizer and precision, and per-task learning curves reveal
471   the resulting capability gains and regressions.

726   A.1   M ODEL , DATA , AND TRAINING PROTOCOL
727
728   The main-study student is Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al., 2025); training uses seeds 42, 43, and 44.
729   The four teachers were trained by reinforcement learning from the same initial model, specializing
730   in mathematics, code, instruction following, and science, respectively. Student and teachers share
731   the tokenizer and native chat template with thinking disabled. The experiment records identify the
732   model revisions and weight files.
733   AdamW configurations use learning rate 2.5 × 10−7 with (β1 , β2 ) = (0.9, 0.98), ϵ = 10−8 , no
734   weight decay or schedule, and global norm-1 clipping. Training sampling uses temperature 1 and
735   top-p = 1, with a 2,048-token prompt cap and 4,096-token response cap. One fresh response is
736   sampled per prompt. Each optimizer update aggregates 64 responses; domain allocation is specified
737   below.
738
      The multi-teacher setup allocates 16 prompts to each domain per update and uses target domain
739   weights 1/4. The single-teacher setup allocates all 64 prompts to the same domain. I64 uses the
740   top-64 objective in Equation 3; I16 uses k = 16.

       When each domain loss is averaged separately, its prompt quota and loss weight control different
1122   quantities. Increasing the quota at fixed λd changes the number of sampled responses and the vari-
1123   ance of the gradient estimate, without directly increasing the domain’s expected contribution to the
1124   total gradient. Mathematics-only runs receive four times as many mathematics prompts per up-
1125   date as joint runs. Their score differences reflect changes in both teacher count and mathematics
1126   exposure.
1127
       Candidate-set coverage also scales the distillation gradient. For the student candidate set S and
1128   its intersection I with the teacher set in Equation 3, the retained student weights sum to r(h) =
1129   pθ (I | h)/pθ (S | h). At a fixed prefix with a nonempty intersection and detached coefficients,
1130   the I64 gradient equals r(h) times the gradient obtained by renormalizing the same weights over
1131   I. Coverage therefore changes gradient scale across prefixes even under equal response weighting.
1132   Measuring the retained mass and empty-intersection rate by domain can reveal unequal coverage
1133   under a fixed candidate size. Enlarging the candidate set or renormalizing its intersection changes
       the objective and requires a separate performance comparison.
