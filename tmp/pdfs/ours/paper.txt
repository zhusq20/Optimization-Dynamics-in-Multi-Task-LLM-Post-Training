009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011
               Multi-task LLM post-training uses task weights to specify capability priorities and
012
               task sampling to allocate compute. Conflating the two makes efficiency claims
013            hard to interpret; global token averaging can also give long-response tasks unin-
014            tended weight.
015
               We present Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) for multi-teacher on-
016
               policy distillation and multi-task reinforcement learning. Each update uses a task-
017            homogeneous batch and gives every response equal weight. GPAS measures task
018            gradients in a metric induced by the optimizer: it recovers raw-gradient sampling
019            for SGD, while our LLM instantiation uses AdamW’s stored second moment.
020            GPAS samples high-impact tasks more often and rescales every sampled gradient
021            by its target weight divided by its sampling probability. The conditional expected
022            gradient remains equal to the user-specified task mixture. The online sampler
023            stores one score per task, and Cost-GPAS also uses measured task time.
024            In a controlled three-task experiment, GPAS attains a calculated error ratio of
025            0.698 and a Monte Carlo ratio of 0.697 relative to uniform sampling; the cost-
026            aware proposal lowers its local compute proxy by 10.7%. The completed ex-
027            periment verifies the estimator calculation. Four-teacher MOPD and three-task
028            GRPO experiments will test the AdamW score, online tracking, teacher compati-
029
               bility, and progress per token and GPU hour. The large-model experiments remain
               pending in this draft.

033   1   I NTRODUCTION
034
035   Post-training one model on mathematics, code, instruction following, and science requires two de-
036   cisions. Target weights state which capabilities the objective values. Sampling probabilities decide
037   which task receives the next rollout, teacher call, and update. Keeping target weights and sampling
038   probabilities separate makes both decisions visible.
039
      Many systems use the sampling ratio as the task weight. Sampling a weak or cheap task more often
040
      then changes the model objective, so the resulting capability trade-off can come from the objective,
041   the compute schedule, or both. A coupled design makes optimization efficiency difficult to measure.
042
043   Response length creates a second ambiguity. In a prompt-balanced mixed batch, global token aver-
044
      aging gives long-response tasks a larger coefficient. Recent MOPD work diagnoses this imbalance
      and changes domain loss weights (Gao et al., 2026). Our training objective gives each response
045
      equal weight inside a task-homogeneous batch.
046
047   Teacher selection is a prerequisite for OPD. A wide teacher–student gap can create extreme token
048   log-ratios, whereas a narrow gap supplies little signal. Recent studies report stronger transfer at
049   moderate distributional overlap (Ma et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b; Ma et al., 2026b). We train
050
      teachers from the student initialization, audit them on student rollouts, and study task allocation only
      within the selected teacher set.
051
052   Suppose task i has target weight λi and sampling probability qi . Multiplying its sampled gradient by
053   λi /qi keeps the conditional mean equal to the target mixture. The sampling probabilities can then
      allocate compute while the target mixture stays fixed.

054
      Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) applies classical importance sampling at the task-
055   batch level. GPAS scores task gradients in a metric induced by the training optimizer, then assigns
056   more probability to tasks with larger scores. The identity metric recovers raw-gradient sampling
057   for SGD; for AdamW, we use the lagged preconditioner from its stored second moment. A moving
058   average estimates each task score from completed updates. A probability floor limits stale scores
059   and large importance weights. Cost-GPAS also uses measured task time.
060
      The controlled experiment checks the sampling formulas with known gradient distributions and
061   costs. The large-model experiments use frozen gradient banks to compare raw gradients, GPAS
062   scores, and measured AdamW task steps. Paired training runs then measure teacher-loss progress
063   per token and GPU hour. A GRPO experiment tests the same task sampler with reward-based
064   updates.
065
      The paper makes three contributions:
066
067          • We define a response-normalized task-batch interface that separates a fixed multi-task target
068            from compute allocation in on-policy post-training.
069
             • We derive task allocation in an optimizer-induced metric, recovering raw-gradient sampling
070
               for SGD and a lagged-preconditioned rule for AdamW. A cost-aware version also uses
071            measured end-to-end task time, and both samplers estimate their scores online.
072
             • We validate the estimator calculation and specify direct MOPD and GRPO tests that con-
073
               nect score quality to progress per token and GPU hour.
074
075   Section 3 defines the objective and sampling interface; Section 4 presents GPAS; Section 5 gives the
076   MOPD and GRPO studies. The appendix contains proofs and implementation details.

      2   R ELATED W ORK
080
081
      Multi-task optimization. Multi-task methods often learn task weights or modify gradient direc-
      tions to improve a capability trade-off (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Chen
082
      et al., 2018). GPAS starts from task weights supplied by the user. GPAS changes the frequency of
083
      task batches and keeps the weighted gradient fixed in expectation. A fixed target makes optimization
084   efficiency measurable under one capability objective.
085
086   Multi-task RL for language models. Joint RL post-training can show uneven gradients and un-
087   even task progress (Ramesh et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Difficulty-based curricula sample weak
088   or slowly improving tasks more often (Corrado et al., 2026). Multi-Task GRPO learns robustness-
089   oriented task weights and preserves its realized batch ratios (Ramesh et al., 2026). Our experiments
090   keep the target task weights fixed. A corrected gap sampler measures the value of a loss-gap signal
091   under the same target objective. An uncorrected version measures the capability shift caused by
092   changing task priorities.
093
094   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD routes student rollouts to domain teachers and trains
095   one shared student (Ma et al., 2026a). Related systems use task-specific teacher interfaces and
096
      language-specialized teachers (Ma et al., 2026b; Xie et al., 2026). Open-MOPD identifies response
      length, teacher–student gap, and reused rewards as sources of capability imbalance and changes
097
      domain loss weights (Gao et al., 2026). Other OPD studies report unreliable token guidance un-
098
      der large teacher–student distribution mismatch (Wang et al., 2026b;a). Same-origin teachers and
099   moderate next-token overlap improve training stability while preserving a useful learning signal (Ma
100   et al., 2026a;b). Our MOPD protocol uses four same-origin teachers, task-homogeneous fixed-token
101   batches, and one optimizer use per rollout. GPAS changes the frequency of complete task batches
102   and applies an inverse sampling factor. Cost-GPAS adds measured GPU time for generation, teacher
103   evaluation, and model updates.
104
105   Importance sampling for stochastic optimization. Sampling examples from their gradient size
106   and applying an inverse probability factor can reduce stochastic-gradient variance (Alain et al., 2015;
107   Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). Cost-aware SGD further samples in proportion to gradient scale di-
      vided by the square root of evaluation cost and has been applied to GRPO (Mohri et al., 2026). Non-

108
      uniform sampling can interact nonlinearly with adaptive optimizers (Lahire, 2023). PiKE adapts
109   data-mixture weights using gradient magnitude, variance, and interactions (Li et al., 2025).
110
111   Cost-Aware GRPO decides which already-generated sequences enter policy updates. GPAS decides
112
      which task receives the next rollout, teacher call, and optimizer step while preserving a user-specified

290   5.2                          F OUR - TEACHER MOPD SETUP
291
292   The planned MOPD study uses a frozen revision of Qwen3-1.7B (Qwen Team, 2025). Four teachers
293   start from the Qwen3 checkpoint and receive separate post-training on mathematics, code, instruc-
294   tion following, and science question answering. Teacher selection occurs before the multi-teacher
295   runs. The release specification requires model revisions, teacher hashes, data splits, and the four
296
      capability scores of every teacher.
297   Each optimizer step samples one task, generates a task-homogeneous batch, calls one frozen teacher,
298   and consumes the rollout once. Every task batch targets 65,536 valid response tokens. Prompt count
299   is estimated from earlier response lengths, and every completed response enters the gradient. The
300   logs record the actual token count and overshoot.
301   The primary loss uses the sampled-token OPD gradient in Appendix B.1. Each response is averaged
302   by its valid-token count, followed by an average over responses and one shared task-gradient clip.
303   The trainer records the raw and AdamW-scaled scores, applies λi /qi , and calls AdamW. The target
304   mixture is λi = 1/4.
305
      All methods use 600 optimizer steps, an 8,192-token response cap, temperature 1, top-p 1, and
306   disabled thinking mode. A uniform pilot fixes the learning rate, warmup, clipping threshold, batch
307   target, moving-average rate γ = 0.95, and probability floor qi ≥ 0.05. Every paired seed shares a
308   16-step round-robin warm start, model state, AdamW state, task cursors, and random streams.

      6     D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
412
413
414   Multi-task post-training needs separate controls for the target objective and compute allocation.
415   Response-normalized task batches define the unit of the target; inverse sampling preserves its con-
416
      ditional mean. GPAS chooses proposals in an optimizer-induced metric, and Cost-GPAS also uses
      measured task time. The identity metric recovers the SGD gradient-norm rule; our LLM experiments
417
      focus on the lagged AdamW metric.
418
419   The controlled experiment supports the local estimator claim. GPAS reduces the measured error
420   by 30.3%, and Cost-GPAS reduces the time-weighted proxy by 10.7%. The constructed compari-
421   son shows that coordinate scale can matter. The planned gradient-bank and exact-step audits will
422
      measure whether the lagged AdamW score remains useful in LLM training.
423   Three boundaries guide interpretation. First, the correction preserves the conditional mean of the
424   gradient delivered to the optimizer. Stateful optimizers can still produce different parameter paths
425   under different task orders. Second, the score combines mean scale and batch variation. Compat-
426   ibility and gradient-bank audits measure whether a large score carries a consistent training signal.
427   Third, Cost-GPAS optimizes an uncentered local compute score. The GPU-hour curves provide the
428
      end-to-end efficiency measurement.
429   The present draft contains controlled estimator evidence and a fixed large-model protocol. Com-
430   pletion of E1–E4 is required for a claim about MOPD training efficiency, and E5 is required for a
431   GRPO transfer claim. The evidence boundary keeps the mathematical result, the MOPD hypothesis,
      and the eventual capability results distinct.
