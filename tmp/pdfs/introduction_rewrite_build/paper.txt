011                                               A BSTRACT
012
013            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) consolidates specialized teacher ca-
014            pabilities into a shared student, yet how training design affects capability inte-
015            gration remains poorly understood. We study three core choices in MOPD: loss
016
               averaging, parameter-update sparsity, and vocabulary supervision, using 1.7B and
               3B LLMs across mathematics, coding, question answering, and instruction fol-
017
               lowing. For loss averaging, global-token, domain-token, and domain-response
018
               averaging objectives show a trade-off between mean performance and domain ca-
019            pability retention: global-token achieves the highest mean performance, while
020            domain-response better preserves the domain capability. For sparsity, we find that
021            parameter updates are sparse in BF16 but dense in FP32, and updates induced by
022            different teachers are strongly aligned and overlapping, challenging the view that
023            specialized capabilities occupy disjoint parameter subsets. For vocabulary super-
024            vision, different produce similar per-batch gradient sparsity but different task out-
025            comes: top-k supervision outperforms sampled-token supervision on three of four
026            domains in MOPD training, while single-teacher training retains cross-domain
027
               trade-offs. We further design a practical MOPD recipe combining supervision
               weighting, capability-aware vocabulary supervision, and checkpoint selection un-
028
               der per-domain retention constraints. [siqi: how much performance we achieved.]

      1   I NTRODUCTION
032
033
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) is increasingly used in industrial LLM post-training to
034
      consolidate specialized capabilities into a single model. Recent examples include Xiaomi’s MiMo-
035
      V2-Flash (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026), NVIDIA’s Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA, 2026), Kimi
036   K3 (Kimi Team et al., 2026), and DeepSeek-V4 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026). In this paradigm, spe-
037   cialized teachers provide token-level feedback on responses generated by the student itself, allowing
038   independently developed experts to transfer their capabilities to a shared student. This design sup-
039   ports capability integration across diverse domains while adapting the supervision to the student’s
040   evolving behavior.
041
      Recent work has advanced both the practice and analysis of this approach. Ma et al. (2026) demon-
042   strate capability integration using policy-gradient and top-k distillation losses. Open-MOPD iden-
043   tifies uneven capability transfer and develops mechanisms for balancing domain token shares, al-
044   locating supervision according to student–teacher gaps, and refreshing student-dependent rewards
045   (Gao et al., 2026). The Nemotron 3 Ultra report further finds that top-k and full-vocabulary match-
046   ing do not outperform sampled-token supervision in its preliminary experiments (NVIDIA, 2026,
047   Section 3.3.5). Complementary studies characterize the sparsity and geometry of OPD parameter
048   changes, including their concentration at checkpoint precision and their evolution during training
049   (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). These findings motivate examining how supervision choices
050
      translate into parameter changes and capability outcomes in a shared student.
051   Several distinctions are central to this examination. Balancing domain weights still leaves open how
052   responses of different lengths are weighted within each domain. Concentrated changes in stored
053   model weights do not, by themselves, describe the breadth of optimizer updates. Likewise, super-
      vising more vocabulary alternatives need not produce a proportional increase in changed parameters,


                                                        1

054
      and similar local update concentration may accompany different capability outcomes. We therefore
055   ask: how do loss averaging and vocabulary supervision affect learning in MOPD, and what do
056   parameter-change measurements reveal about these choices?
057
058
      We investigate this question through controlled comparisons with a Qwen3-1.7B student and four
      domain teachers covering mathematics, code, instruction following, and science. We combine al-
059
      gebraic analysis of loss averaging, local measurements at fixed student and optimizer states, and
060
      capability evaluations along training. Our contributions are threefold:
061
062         1. Separating domain balancing from response weighting (Section 3). We distinguish
063            global-token, domain-token, and domain-response averaging and show that within-domain
064            length weighting changes gradient direction even when domain weights are identical. At
065            the common update-50 comparison, global-token averaging achieves the highest mean
066            score, while domain-response averaging yields the smallest worst-domain decline from
067
               initialization. The choice therefore depends on the intended capability tradeoff.
068         2. Characterizing parameter changes across numerical representations (Section 4). We
069            measure individual optimizer steps and cumulative changes separately in FP32 optimizer
070            weights and BF16 model weights. At update 250, cumulative changes in the two joint-
071
               training branches are nonzero in 5.9–7.1% of BF16 coordinates and 95.7–96.1% of FP32
               coordinates. These measurements clarify how sparse stored changes coexist with broad
072
               optimization activity in MOPD.
073
074
            3. Comparing vocabulary supervision through both updates and capability (Section 5).
               At the tested shared state, sampled-token, top-k, and full-vocabulary losses produce similar
075
               local update concentration. Online training nevertheless yields different capability profiles:
076
               in four-teacher training at update 250, student–teacher top-64 intersection (I64) supervision
077            scores higher than sampled-token supervision in three domains and ties on instruction fol-
078            lowing. The mathematics-only teacher setting retains cross-domain tradeoffs, motivating
079            evaluation beyond local sparsity alone.
080
081   We use these findings to outline a candidate MOPD recipe combining explicit supervision weights,
082   vocabulary feedback selected using capability evaluations, and checkpoint selection under per-
083   domain retention constraints (Section 6). Its combined effectiveness and stability remain to be
084
      validated.

403   With the retention goal fixed, the averaging rule should express the intended importance of each
404   training response. For an objective that weights responses equally within each domain, we use DR
405

      domain provide the starting allocation. If longer responses are deliberately assigned more weight
406
      within a domain, DT expresses that alternative. Under either reduction, the prompt quota and the
407

408   separately, increasing a quota at fixed λd changes exposure and estimation variance without directly
409   increasing that domain’s expected gradient weight. Generated lengths and truncation rates should
410   therefore be recorded alongside both quantities when diagnosing an imbalance.
411
      Once these weights are explicit, vocabulary feedback can be chosen using its capability profile
412
      and computational cost. The joint comparison in Section 5 motivates I64 as a candidate alongside
413
      DR, with PG retained as a reference. Its feedback scale also deserves attention. For the student
414   candidate set S and teacher intersection I in Equation 9, the retained student weights sum to r(h) =
415   pθ (I | h)/pθ (S | h). At a fixed prefix with a nonempty intersection and detached coefficients, the
416   I64 gradient is r(h) times the gradient obtained by renormalizing the same weights over I. Thus
417   removing length weighting does not remove variation in feedback scale across prefixes. Monitoring
418   this retained mass and empty intersections by domain helps assess whether a fixed candidate size
419   supplies comparable coverage; increasing or renormalizing a small intersection requires validation
420   because it can also amplify unreliable feedback.
421   If a domain repeatedly falls below its retention tolerance, the response should depend on the diag-
422   nosed cause. Exposure, length, and coverage checks can reveal inadequate sampling or unintended
423   weighting. When these do not explain the decline, teacher interactions should be examined at a
424   common student and optimizer state. The overlap diagnostic defined in Section 4 describes shared
425   update locations; a further functional check is to apply each teacher’s proposed step and measure
426   the change in fixed probe losses from all domains. A zero-current-gradient step provides a reference
427   for the contribution of optimizer history. This comparison can identify local cross-domain harm
428
      that coordinate overlap alone does not establish, while subsequent capability evaluations determine
      whether it persists along training. Gradient modification or task-specific parameters become inter-
429
      ventions to test when such harm remains reproducible after the allocation and coverage checks.

      7   R ELATED W ORK
449
450   MOPD combines specialized teachers through feedback on student-generated responses (Ma et al.,
451   2026). Open-MOPD studies capability imbalance and introduces token-share balancing, gap-based
452   weighting, and reward refresh (Gao et al., 2026). Its token-share correction maps directly to domain-
453   token averaging under the same batch and target domain weights. Our first study separates this
454   between-domain correction from within-domain response weighting and compares their capability
455
      outcomes.
456   Mukherjee et al. (2025) investigate small parameter subsets that support reinforcement-learning fine-
457   tuning. Yu et al. (2026) find concentrated checkpoint changes under dense distillation supervision
458   and overlap between the changes produced by different teachers in separately trained students. Our
459   second question concerns teachers acting from the same joint student and optimizer state. Our third
460   compares vocabulary supervision through both parameter concentration and capability. Shen et al.
461
      (2026) study OPD parameter geometry and select response positions for learning; their position
      selection concerns a different supervision dimension from the vocabulary alternatives scored at each
462
      prefix here.

467
468   The experiments cover one model family and one training seed. The local supervision compari-
469   son uses four prefix banks with high top-k probability coverage, limiting what it establishes about
470   broader vocabulary tails or other student states. The bootstrap intervals quantify evaluation-question
471
      uncertainty at fixed checkpoints, not variability across training runs. These limits apply to the pro-
      posed recipe as well as to the individual comparisons.
472
473   Local changes in a distillation loss and long-term capability transfer remain distinct measurements.
474   Student-generated responses evolve during training, so a fixed-state diagnostic does not capture the
475   full trajectory. The recipe in Section 6 uses such diagnostics to guide decisions, with stability left to
476   prospective tests of domain retention under matched budgets and repeated training runs.
