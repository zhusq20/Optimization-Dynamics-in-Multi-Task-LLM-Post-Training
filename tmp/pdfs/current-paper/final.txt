009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011
               Multi-task LLM post-training uses task weights to specify capability priorities and
012
               task sampling to allocate compute. Conflating the two makes efficiency claims
013            hard to interpret; global token averaging can also give long-response tasks unin-
014            tended weight.
015
               We present Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) for multi-teacher on-
016
               policy distillation and multi-task reinforcement learning. Each update uses a task-
017            homogeneous batch and gives every response equal weight. GPAS measures task
018            gradients after scaling each parameter coordinate by AdamW’s stored second mo-
019            ment. GPAS samples high-impact tasks more often and rescales every sampled
020            gradient by its target weight divided by its sampling probability. The conditional
021            expected gradient remains equal to the user-specified task mixture. The online
022            sampler reuses completed gradients and stores one score per task. Cost-GPAS
023            also uses measured task time.
024            In a controlled three-task experiment, GPAS attains a calculated error ratio of
025            0.698 and a Monte Carlo ratio of 0.697 relative to uniform sampling; the cost-
026            aware proposal lowers its local compute proxy by 10.7%. The completed ex-
027            periment verifies the estimator calculation. Four-teacher MOPD and three-task
028            GRPO experiments will test the AdamW score, online tracking, teacher compati-
029
               bility, and progress per token and GPU hour. The large-model experiments remain
               pending in this draft.

033   1   I NTRODUCTION
034
035   Post-training one model on mathematics, code, instruction following, and science requires two de-
036   cisions. Target weights state which capabilities the objective values. Sampling probabilities decide
037   which task receives the next rollout, teacher call, and update. Keeping target weights and sampling
038   probabilities separate makes both decisions visible.
039
      Many systems use the sampling ratio as the task weight. Sampling a weak or cheap task more often
040
      then changes the model objective, so the resulting capability trade-off can come from the objective,
041   the compute schedule, or both. A coupled design makes optimization efficiency difficult to measure.
042
043   Response length creates a second ambiguity. In a prompt-balanced mixed batch, global token aver-
044
      aging gives long-response tasks a larger coefficient. Recent MOPD work diagnoses this imbalance
      and changes domain loss weights (Gao et al., 2026). Our training objective gives each response
045
      equal weight inside a task-homogeneous batch.
046
047   Teacher selection is a prerequisite for OPD. A wide teacher–student gap can create extreme token
048   log-ratios, whereas a narrow gap supplies little signal. Recent studies report stronger transfer at
049   moderate distributional overlap (Ma et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b; Ma et al., 2026b). We train
050
      teachers from the student initialization, audit them on student rollouts, and study task allocation only
      within the selected teacher set.
051
052   Suppose task i has target weight λi and sampling probability qi . Multiplying its sampled gradient by
053   λi /qi keeps the conditional mean equal to the target mixture. The sampling probabilities can then
      allocate compute while the target mixture stays fixed.

054
      Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) applies classical importance sampling at the task-
055   batch level. GPAS first scales task gradients with AdamW’s stored second moment, then assigns
056   more probability to tasks with larger scaled gradients. A moving average estimates each task score
057   from completed updates. A probability floor limits stale scores and large importance weights. Cost-
058   GPAS also uses measured task time.
059
      The controlled experiment checks the sampling formulas with known gradient distributions and
060
      costs. The large-model experiments use frozen gradient banks to compare raw gradients, GPAS
061   scores, and measured AdamW task steps. Paired training runs then measure teacher-loss progress
062   per token and GPU hour. A GRPO experiment tests the same task sampler with reward-based
063   updates.
064
      The paper makes three contributions:
065
066          • We define a response-normalized task-batch interface that separates a fixed multi-task target
067            from compute allocation in on-policy post-training.
068          • We use AdamW-scaled task gradients to allocate updates. A cost-aware version also uses
069            measured end-to-end task time. Both samplers estimate their task scores online from com-
070            pleted updates.
071          • We validate the estimator calculation and specify direct MOPD and GRPO tests that con-
072            nect score quality to progress per token and GPU hour.
073
074   Section 3 defines the objective and sampling interface; Section 4 presents GPAS; Section 5 gives the
075   MOPD and GRPO studies. The appendix contains proofs and implementation details.

077   2   R ELATED W ORK
078
079   Multi-task optimization. Multi-task methods often learn task weights or modify gradient direc-
080   tions to improve a capability trade-off (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Chen
081   et al., 2018). GPAS starts from task weights supplied by the user. GPAS changes the frequency of
082   task batches and keeps the weighted gradient fixed in expectation. A fixed target makes optimization
083   efficiency measurable under one capability objective.
084
085
      Multi-task RL for language models. Joint RL post-training can show uneven gradients and un-
      even task progress (Ramesh et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Difficulty-based curricula sample weak
086
      or slowly improving tasks more often (Corrado et al., 2026). Multi-Task GRPO learns robustness-
087
      oriented task weights and preserves its realized batch ratios (Ramesh et al., 2026). Our experiments
088   keep the target task weights fixed. A corrected gap sampler measures the value of a loss-gap signal
089   under the same target objective. An uncorrected version measures the capability shift caused by
090   changing task priorities.
091
092   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD routes student rollouts to domain teachers and trains
093   one shared student (Ma et al., 2026a). Related systems use task-specific teacher interfaces and
094   language-specialized teachers (Ma et al., 2026b; Xie et al., 2026). Open-MOPD identifies response
095   length, teacher–student gap, and reused rewards as sources of capability imbalance and changes
096   domain loss weights (Gao et al., 2026). Other OPD studies report unreliable token guidance un-
097
      der large teacher–student distribution mismatch (Wang et al., 2026b;a). Same-origin teachers and
      moderate next-token overlap improve training stability while preserving a useful learning signal (Ma
098
      et al., 2026a;b). Our MOPD protocol uses four same-origin teachers, task-homogeneous fixed-token
099
      batches, and one optimizer use per rollout. GPAS changes the frequency of complete task batches
100   and applies an inverse sampling factor. Cost-GPAS adds measured GPU time for generation, teacher
101   evaluation, and model updates.
102
103   Importance sampling for stochastic optimization. Sampling examples from their gradient size
104   and applying an inverse probability factor can reduce stochastic-gradient variance (Alain et al., 2015;
105   Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). Cost-aware SGD further samples in proportion to gradient scale di-
106   vided by the square root of evaluation cost and has been applied to GRPO (Mohri et al., 2026). Non-
107   uniform sampling can interact nonlinearly with adaptive optimizers (Lahire, 2023). PiKE adapts
      data-mixture weights using gradient magnitude, variance, and interactions (Li et al., 2025).

      5.2   F OUR - TEACHER MOPD SETUP
271
272   The planned MOPD study uses a frozen revision of Qwen3-1.7B (Qwen Team, 2025). Four teachers
273   start from the Qwen3 checkpoint and receive separate post-training on mathematics, code, instruc-
274   tion following, and science question answering. Teacher selection occurs before the multi-teacher
275   runs. The release specification requires model revisions, teacher hashes, data splits, and the four
276   capability scores of every teacher.
277   Each optimizer step samples one task, generates a task-homogeneous batch, calls one frozen teacher,
278   and consumes the rollout once. Every task batch targets 65,536 valid response tokens. Prompt count
279   is estimated from earlier response lengths, and every completed response enters the gradient. The
280   logs record the actual token count and overshoot.
281
      The primary loss uses the sampled-token OPD gradient in Appendix B.2. Each response is averaged
282   by its valid-token count, followed by an average over responses and one shared task-gradient clip.
283   The trainer records the raw and AdamW-scaled scores, applies λi /qi , and calls AdamW. The target
284   mixture is λi = 1/4.
285
      All methods use 600 optimizer steps, an 8,192-token response cap, temperature 1, top-p 1, and
286
      disabled thinking mode. A uniform pilot fixes the learning rate, warmup, clipping threshold, batch
287
      target, moving-average rate γ = 0.95, and probability floor qi ≥ 0.05. Every paired seed shares a
288   16-step round-robin warm start, model state, AdamW state, task cursors, and random streams.

386   6   D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
387
388   Multi-task post-training needs separate controls for the target objective and compute allocation.
389   Response-normalized task batches define the unit of the target; inverse sampling preserves its con-
390   ditional mean. GPAS chooses proposals from a lagged optimizer metric, and Cost-GPAS also uses
391   measured task time.
392
      The controlled experiment supports the local estimator claim. GPAS reduces the measured error
393   by 30.3%, and Cost-GPAS reduces the time-weighted proxy by 10.7%. The constructed compari-
394   son shows that coordinate scale can matter. The planned gradient-bank and exact-step audits will
395   measure whether the lagged AdamW score remains useful in LLM training.
396
      Three boundaries guide interpretation. First, the correction preserves the conditional mean of the
397
      gradient delivered to AdamW. AdamW’s moving averages can still produce different parameter
398
      paths under different task orders. Second, the score combines mean scale and batch variation. Com-
399   patibility and gradient-bank audits measure whether a large score carries a consistent training signal.
400   Third, Cost-GPAS optimizes an uncentered local compute score. The GPU-hour curves provide the
401   end-to-end efficiency measurement.
402
      The present draft contains controlled estimator evidence and a fixed large-model protocol. Com-
403
      pletion of E1–E4 is required for a claim about MOPD training efficiency, and E5 is required for a
404
      GRPO transfer claim. The evidence boundary keeps the mathematical result, the MOPD hypothesis,
405   and the eventual capability results distinct.

417   the reported capability scores. We record data provenance, invalid outputs, and verifier failures to
418   support careful interpretation of the results.

519   valid-token target, and record prompt count, token count, and GPU time.
