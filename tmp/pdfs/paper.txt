009                                             A BSTRACT
010
011
              Multi-task LLM post-training uses task weights to define capability priorities and
012           task sampling to allocate compute. A trainer that averages every token in a mixed
013           batch gives longer-response tasks more weight. Uniform task sampling also ig-
014           nores differences in gradient size and GPU time.
015
              We present Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) for multi-teacher on-
016           policy distillation (MOPD) and multi-task reinforcement learning. GPAS uses
017           task-homogeneous batches and averages each response by its valid-token count.
018           The sampler assigns more batches to tasks with larger AdamW-scaled gradient
019           scores. Multiplying each sampled gradient by its target weight divided by its
020           sampling probability preserves the target gradient in expectation. Cost-GPAS also
021           divides the score by the square root of measured task time. Both methods reuse
022           gradients and timing records from completed training steps, so the sampler adds
023           zero forward or backward passes.
024           A controlled three-task experiment gives a calculated GPAS error ratio of 0.698
025           and a Monte Carlo ratio of 0.697, relative to uniform sampling. Cost-GPAS re-
026           duces the time-weighted second-moment proxy by 10.7%. The controlled results
027
              verify the sampling calculation. We also give a four-teacher MOPD and three-task
              GRPO protocol that measures whether the local advantage improves teacher loss
028
              per token and per GPU hour. The large-model runs remain pending in this draft.

032   1   I NTRODUCTION
033
034   Post-training one language model on mathematics, code, instruction following, and science requires
035   two decisions. Task weights state which capabilities the training objective values. Task sampling
036   decides which task receives the next rollout, teacher call, and optimizer update. A clear training
037   specification exposes both decisions because they answer different questions.
038   Many systems use the sampling ratio as the task weight. Increasing the sampling rate of a weak
039   or inexpensive task then changes the model objective. The final capability trade-off can come from
040   the new weights, the compute schedule, or both. Coupling the objective and sampler makes an
041   efficiency claim difficult to interpret.
042   Response length creates a second source of ambiguity. A mixed batch with equal prompt counts can
043   contain very different token counts for different tasks. Global token averaging gives long-response
044   tasks a larger coefficient. Recent MOPD work reports length-driven token imbalance and improves
045   concise capabilities by changing domain loss weights (Gao et al., 2026). We take a fixed-objective
046   route. Each optimizer step uses one task-homogeneous batch, and each response contributes its
047   mean token gradient. Response normalization gives every response an equal coefficient inside its
048   task batch.
049   Teacher selection sets an additional condition for OPD. A wide teacher–student distribution gap can
050   create extreme token log-ratios. A narrow gap supplies a weak training signal. Recent OPD studies
051   report stronger transfer when teacher and student reasoning distributions have moderate overlap (Ma
052   et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b; Ma et al., 2026b). The MOPD protocol trains every teacher from
053   the shared student initialization and audits compatibility on student rollouts. GPAS studies task
      allocation inside the selected teacher set.

054
      After defining a comparable task gradient, we ask how to spend a limited compute budget. Suppose
055   task i has target weight λi and sampling probability qi . Multiplying its gradient by λi /qi keeps the
056   expected gradient equal to the target mixture. The sampler can visit high-score tasks more often
057   while retaining the stated capability priorities.
058
      Gradient-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (GPAS) estimates each task’s gradient size under the coor-
059
      dinate scale stored by AdamW. A larger score gives a task more sampling probability because its
060
      omission creates more local estimation error. Cost-GPAS divides the GPAS score by the square root
061   of measured task time. Both methods use one moving-average rate and one probability floor.
062
063   The executable controlled experiment first checks the estimator formulas with known task distri-
064
      butions, coordinate scales, and costs. The planned large-model study uses four frozen teachers for
      mathematics, code, instruction following, and science question answering. Frozen gradient banks
065
      measure mean-gradient error, and paired training runs measure teacher-loss progress per gener-
066
      ated token and per GPU hour. A GRPO study applies the sampler to reward-based gradients. The
067   evaluation order separates a verified local claim from the empirical question of end-to-end training
068   efficiency.

      2   R ELATED W ORK
085
086
      Multi-task optimization. Multi-task methods often learn task weights or modify gradient direc-
087   tions to improve a capability trade-off (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Chen
088   et al., 2018). GPAS starts from task weights supplied by the user. GPAS changes the frequency of
089   task batches and keeps the weighted gradient fixed in expectation. A fixed target makes optimization
090   efficiency measurable under one capability objective.
091
092   Multi-task RL for language models. Joint RL post-training can show uneven gradients and un-
093   even task progress (Ramesh et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Difficulty-based curricula sample weak
094   or slowly improving tasks more often (Corrado et al., 2026). Multi-Task GRPO learns robustness-
095   oriented task weights and preserves its realized batch ratios (Ramesh et al., 2026). Our fixed target
096   provides a controlled comparison: a corrected gap sampler measures the value of a loss-gap signal,
097
      and its uncorrected version measures the effect of changing task priorities.
098
099
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD routes student rollouts to domain teachers and trains
      one shared student (Ma et al., 2026a). Related systems use task-specific teacher interfaces and
100
      language-specialized teachers (Ma et al., 2026b; Xie et al., 2026). Open-MOPD identifies response
101
      length, teacher–student gap, and reused rewards as sources of capability imbalance and changes
102   domain loss weights (Gao et al., 2026). Other OPD studies report unreliable token guidance un-
103   der large teacher–student distribution mismatch (Wang et al., 2026b;a). Same-origin teachers and
104   moderate next-token overlap improve training stability while preserving a useful learning signal (Ma
105   et al., 2026a;b). Our MOPD protocol uses four same-origin teachers, task-homogeneous fixed-token
106   batches, and one optimizer use per rollout. GPAS changes the frequency of complete task batches
107   and applies an inverse sampling factor. Cost-GPAS adds measured GPU time for generation, teacher
      evaluation, and model updates.

      Importance sampling for stochastic optimization. Sampling examples from their gradient size
109   and applying an inverse probability factor can reduce stochastic-gradient variance (Alain et al., 2015;
110   Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). PiKE adapts large-scale data mixtures using task-gradient magni-
111   tude, variance, and interaction information (Li et al., 2025). GPAS applies gradient-size sampling
112   to a complete post-training batch, including generation, teacher or reward evaluation, and one opti-
113   mizer step. Its online score uses the gradient and AdamW state already available after that batch.

      Cost-GPAS applies the moving-average coefficient γ to measured batch time. A short round-robin
217   warm start supplies one observation for every task. A probability floor controls stale scores and
218   large inverse sampling factors. At each later step, the trainer forms q, samples one task, computes

224   changed. Section 5.4 tests online score tracking, and Section 5.5 tests whether the lower estimator

370   6     D ISCUSSION AND C ONCLUSION
371
372   Multi-task post-training needs separate controls for capability priorities and compute allocation.
373   Response-normalized task batches give each response a fixed coefficient. GPAS then samples tasks
374
      from their AdamW-scaled gradient size and uses an inverse sampling factor to preserve the target
      mean gradient. Cost-GPAS also uses measured task time.
375
376   The controlled experiment supports the local estimator claim. GPAS reduces the measured error
377   by 30.3%, and Cost-GPAS reduces the time-weighted proxy by 10.7%. The comparison with raw-
      gradient sampling also shows why the AdamW coordinate scale matters in the constructed problem.

      Three boundaries guide interpretation. First, the correction preserves the mean gradient delivered to
379   AdamW; optimizer moments and task order still affect the parameter path. Second, the score com-
380   bines mean-gradient size and batch variation. The teacher-compatibility and gradient-bank audits
381   separate mean scale from batch variation. Third, the Cost-GPAS objective is a local second-moment
382   proxy. The paired GPU-hour curves measure actual training efficiency.
383
      The present draft contains controlled estimator evidence and a fixed large-model protocol. Com-
384
      pletion of E1–E4 is required for a claim about MOPD training efficiency, and E5 is required for a
385   GRPO transfer claim. The evidence boundary keeps the mathematical result, the MOPD hypothesis,
386   and the eventual capability results distinct.

541
542   For task i, GRPO samples response groups from one prompt, computes within-prompt advantages,
