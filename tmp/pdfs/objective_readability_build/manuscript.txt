011                                              A BSTRACT
012
013            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) aims to consolidate specialized ca-
014            pabilities in a single student. We examine three aspects of its optimization: token
015            balancing, gradient sparsity, and supervision density. Starting from the MOPD
016            learning objective, we first show how loss averaging determines both domain
017            weights and the relative weight of long and short responses. A finite-batch gra-
018            dient decomposition separates these effects and motivates matched experiments
019
               on capability balance. Second, we investigate whether different teachers concen-
               trate their gradients on overlapping parameter subsets within the same MOPD
020
               student, and whether more different teacher distributions correspond to more dif-
021
               ferent subsets. Prior observations of sparse cumulative OPD parameter changes
022            motivate this question but do not establish gradient sparsity; we measure gra-
023            dients, optimizer steps, and cumulative changes separately. Third, we compare
024            sampled-token policy gradients with teacher top-k supervision in single-teacher
025            and joint training, using full-vocabulary reverse-KL gradients at shared prefixes
026            as a local reference. The planned experiments pair gradient concentration with
027            per-domain capability and account for sampling variability, training exposure, and
028            compute. Each question is developed together with its experimental design, with-
029            out presuming that sparse gradients, low teacher overlap, or sampled supervision
030
               improve capability integration. Empirical outcomes remain to be measured.

033   1   I NTRODUCTION
034
035   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) transfers domain specialists into one student by scoring
036   student-generated responses with their assigned teachers (Ma et al., 2026). The objective is to inte-
037   grate their capabilities, but equal numbers of prompts from each domain need not give those domains
038   equal influence on training. Open-MOPD identifies capability imbalance and studies token-share
039   weighting, changing distillation gaps, and stale student-dependent rewards (Gao et al., 2026). These
040   findings motivate examining how the objective weights each domain and which student parameters
041   receive its strongest gradients.
042   Prior work reports sparse cumulative parameter changes in RL (Mukherjee et al., 2025) and OPD
043   (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). Such observations do not establish that instantaneous gradients
044   are sparse: optimizer state and accumulation over training can change both the size and location
045   of parameter movement. We make this distinction explicit and ask how multiple teachers act on a
046   common student during MOPD. Gradients measured in that shared student are more directly relevant
047   to this question than endpoints of separately trained students.
048   After introducing the MOPD learning objective in Section 2, we develop three studies and place
049   each experiment alongside its corresponding question:
050
051        1. Token balancing (Section 3). We derive how global-token, domain-token, and domain-
052           response averaging weight domains and response lengths, then compare their effects on
053           capability balance. The analysis explains existing loss reductions without introducing a
              new allocation algorithm.

170   4     G RADIENT S PARSITY AND T EACHER OVERLAP
171
172   We ask whether OPD gradients concentrate on a small fraction of parameters, whether different
173   teachers affect the same parameters within one MOPD student, and whether teacher distribution
174   differences predict this overlap. Each question includes its planned experiment; no outcome is
175   assumed.
176
177   4.1   D OES GRADIENT SPARSITY PERSIST UNDER MULTIPLE TEACHERS ?
178
179   Prior work reports concentrated parameter changes in RL fine-tuning (Mukherjee et al., 2025) and
180   OPD (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). These observations motivate measuring both gradients and
181
      parameter changes in our setting. For a domain gradient gd = ∇θ Ld , where Ld is that domain’s
      mean loss, we report the fraction of parameters with absolute gradient at most a threshold τ , together
182
      with the fraction of squared gradient norm carried by the largest-magnitude ρ fraction of parameters.
183
      We sweep τ and ρ: “gradient sparsity” here means near-zero gradients or concentrated gradient
184   energy, not necessarily exact zeros.
185
186   Experiment. Using the representative single-teacher and joint runs from Section 5, collect gradi-
187   ents at initialization and early, intermediate, and final checkpoints. Compare each domain’s gradient
188   and the combined MOPD gradient under the same loss reduction, reporting curves against both do-
189   main exposure and compute. Match diagnostic response counts per domain across single-teacher and
190   joint students, report valid-token counts, and check sensitivity to batch size; record the combined
191   gradient’s full batch size separately. Repeat over fresh student-rollout batches, record accumula-
192   tion denominators, and accumulate raw gradients in FP32 before clipping or optimizer processing.
193   Threshold curves, gradient norms, and energy concentration jointly show whether apparent sparsity
194
      reflects small overall scale or concentration on particular parameters.
195   As secondary measurements, record optimizer steps and cumulative changes ∆ = θ − θ0 . These
196   are not equivalent to gradients: AdamW momentum and weight decay can move parameters with
197   a zero current gradient, and changes from successive steps can cancel. Compare FP32 master-
198   weight differences with exported BF16 checkpoint differences to identify rounding-induced zeros;
199   converting BF16 values to FP32 cannot recover lost changes. This comparison checks the reported
200
      parameter-change phenomenon while separately testing gradient sparsity in MOPD.

248   5     D OES S UPERVISION D ENSITY C HANGE G RADIENT S PARSITY ?
249
250   Our third study asks whether vocabulary-level supervision changes how strongly gradients concen-
251   trate on a small fraction of model parameters. Sampled-token PG uses feedback for the generated
252
      token, full-vocabulary reverse KL includes every vocabulary alternative, and teacher top-k provides
      a practical intermediate choice (Section 2). This concerns vocabulary coverage at each prefix, rather
253
      than the number of supervised response positions. Sampled-token feedback does not imply a sparse
254
      parameter gradient: the sampled token’s log probability depends on all logits through the softmax
255   normalizer. Concentrated OPD parameter changes have also been observed with dense feedback (Yu
256   et al., 2026). We therefore test for a difference without assuming that PG produces sparser gradients
257   or better capability integration.

298   5.3   I NTERPRETATION AND DECISION RULE
299
300   At a fixed prefix, fresh unclipped sampled-token PG with a detached log-ratio coefficient has the
301   same expected gradient as full-vocabulary reverse KL (Yu et al., 2026; Oh et al., 2026). This identity
302   holds with fixed prefix weights; weighting an original rollout token by its eventual response length
303   need not preserve it. Clipping and top-k truncation also change the comparison, and equality in
304
      expectation does not equate sampled gradients, nonlinear optimizer steps, or training trajectories.
305   We report paired-seed effect estimates and confidence intervals against a material difference margin
306   specified before examining results. One-seed runs remain exploratory; prompt resampling measures
307   evaluation uncertainty, not training-seed variation. Intervals entirely within the margin support no
308   material sparsity difference in the tested regime; intervals spanning negligible and material effects
309   are inconclusive. Only a stable, meaningful gap motivates the optional sampling-variance and trun-
310
      cation controls in Appendix C.3.

320   conflict also motivates distinguishing weight balance from joint capability gains (Chen et al., 2018;
321   Yu et al., 2020).
322
323   Sparse parameter changes and teacher differences. Mukherjee et al. (2025) report sparse cu-
      mulative parameter changes in RL and repeated changes in similar parameter subsets across settings.

      Yu et al. (2026) establish related OPD signatures, including overlap when varying teachers and ex-
325   periments that restrict training to selected parameters. Shen et al. (2026) examine update subspaces
326   and response-position thinning. These results motivate our study but do not establish sparsity of
327   instantaneous gradients. Teacher compatibility also affects distillation success (Li et al., 2026; Ma

336

337   reserved for interpreting a stable difference; we do not propose a new estimator.

      7   D ISCUSSION
341
      The three studies connect loss weighting, gradient concentration in a shared MOPD student, and
342
      vocabulary-level supervision. Their interpretation depends on distinguishing raw gradients from op-
343
      timizer steps and cumulative parameter changes. Sparse cumulative changes alone do not establish
344   sparse gradients, while overlapping high-magnitude gradients can indicate either shared or opposing
345   corrections.
346
347
      The PG/top-k comparison tests for a material difference rather than assuming an advantage for sam-
      pled supervision. A resolved absence of such a difference is informative within the tested regime;
348
      a stable gap can motivate further analysis. Likewise, teacher distribution differences may or may
349
      not predict which parameters receive strong gradients. Capability measurements determine whether
350   these optimization patterns accompany useful integration.
