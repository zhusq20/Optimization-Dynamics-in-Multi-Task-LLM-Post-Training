009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) is increasingly used to integrate inde-
012            pendently post-trained specialists into one language model. The central difficulty
013            is that one finite-capacity student must absorb several teacher policies through a
014            shared optimizer and a finite training budget.
015            We study the practically typical regime in which a shared student cannot exactly
016            match all routed teachers and finite training does not reach even the best local
017            compromise. Around a checkpoint, we use the standard semi-gradient view of
018            OPD: sampled prefixes are held fixed while the student logits are differentiated.
019            Linearizing these logits and locally quadraticizing reverse KL turns MOPD into a
020            weighted least-squares problem.
021            This view separates the irreducible teacher-fitting residual from the optimization
022            error remaining at the current iterate. Task loss weights can move the best local
023            compromise, whereas task sampling changes the stochastic path toward a fixed
024
               compromise. Because Adam-style optimizers rescale task gradients, we base the
               finite-step analysis on optimizer-preconditioned update sizes rather than raw gra-
025
               dient norms. This leads to Objective-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (OPAS), a
026
               task-level importance-sampling rule that reallocates updates without changing the
027            intended MOPD gradient objective. Its ideal allocation minimizes the second mo-
028            ment of the preconditioned single-task update; full proofs are deferred to the ap-
029            pendix.

032   1   I NTRODUCTION
033
034   Large language models are increasingly post-trained with specialized objectives for reasoning, cod-
035   ing, instruction following, and other capabilities. A natural way to combine independently trained
036   specialists is multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD): prompts from each domain are routed to
037   the corresponding teacher, while a single student is trained to match all teachers (Ma et al., 2026a).
038
      Assigning each question to its corresponding teacher avoids ambiguous supervision, but it does not
039   remove the shared capacity and finite-budget constraints of the student. Recent work observes cross-
040   domain “seesaw” effects when teacher mixtures change (Li et al., 2026b) and substantial imbalance
041   from unequal response lengths and optimization exposure (Gao et al., 2026). We therefore ask:
042
043            How does a shared student compromise among teachers it cannot all fit, and how
044            much of the observed gap is merely unfinished optimization?
045
046   We take imperfect joint fit as the default. Independently post-trained teachers request different
047   function changes from a finite-capacity shared student, so some teacher-fitting residual generally
048   remains. Separately, finite stochastic training may not reach the best compromise even within a
049   local model. Conflating these two gaps makes an allocation problem look like a representational
050
      conflict, or vice versa. Exact simultaneous fit is useful as a limiting sanity check, but it is not our
      operative assumption.
051
052   We formalize this distinction for the semi-gradient update used in practical reverse-KL MOPD.

076   sampling principle (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018); our contribution is to apply
077   it at the routed-task level while keeping the MOPD scalarization explicit.
078
      Our contributions are therefore threefold. First, we formulate semi-gradient MOPD as local multi-
079   teacher fitting and separate irreducible incompatibility from finite-step optimization error; the exact
080   weighted projection is placed in the appendix. Second, we make the finite-step analysis optimizer-
081   aware and test whether preconditioned update scale predicts which tasks lag during training. Third,
082   we turn the distinction between task weighting and task sampling into OPAS, a low-overhead rule
083   for reducing optimization error without changing the intended task weights.

      2   R ELATED W ORK
086
087   Multi-teacher distillation and capability integration. MOPD integrates independently RL-
088   trained domain teachers through routed supervision on student rollouts (Ma et al., 2026a). Recent
089   extensions use task-specific soft-prompt teachers that share the student’s backbone (Ma et al., 2026b)
090   or apply language-routed MOPD to multilingual speech recognition (Xie et al., 2026). Open-MOPD
091   attributes capability imbalance to unequal token counts, teacher–student gaps, and stale rewards, and
092   uses these signals to rebalance training (Gao et al., 2026). Complementary work shows that chang-
093   ing teacher data proportions can produce cross-domain “seesaw” effects even with oracle routing
094   (Li et al., 2026b). Single-teacher analyses additionally show that a large teacher–student distribu-
095
      tion gap can make OPD guidance ineffective or unstable (Li et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b;a). That
      pairwise learnability issue is distinct from our question: teachers can each be learnable in isolation
096
      while their requested changes remain jointly incompatible in one student.
097
098
      Multi-task optimization. Recent work contrasts the gradient geometry of multi-task SFT and RL
099
      (Zhu et al., 2026). We focus instead on two quantities that map directly to MOPD training: the
100   teacher-fitting residual that remains after local optimization, and the task update after the shared
101   optimizer’s preconditioning. This distinction matters for Adam-style training because raw gradient
102   norm need not reflect the size of the parameter update.
103
104   Task weighting and adaptive sampling. Adaptive multi-task methods often change loss weights,
105   for example by balancing gradient magnitudes (Chen et al., 2018), while recent multi-task RL work
106   samples tasks to improve worst-task return (Corrado et al., 2026). Classical importance sampling
107   instead changes the sampling distribution and corrects sampled gradients, preserving the target ob-
      jective while reducing estimator variance (Alain et al., 2015; Katharopoulos & Fleuret, 2018). OPAS

      applies this principle at the routed-task level. This differs from uncorrected gap-following allocation,
109   which also changes the effective teacher mixture.

243
244
      The experiments follow the theory one claim at a time. We use independently trained mathemat-
      ics, code, and instruction-following teachers with one shared student. All comparisons use the
245
      same cached rollout batches, per-task mean-token KL, AdamW state, intended weights w, and to-
246
      tal student/teacher token budget. We report three seeds and confidence intervals. A small exact-fit
247   construction is retained only as an appendix sanity check.

      6.1   D O LOCAL GAPS PREDICT MIXTURE SENSITIVITY ?
250
251   At several checkpoints, we freeze one rollout batch and approximately solve equation 6 with
252   Jacobian–vector products, restricting the solution to the norm of the next short-horizon update. This
                                                                          ⋆
253   gives FitGap, OptGap, and the per-task residual-gradient norm ∥ri,w    ∥. From the same checkpoint
254   and optimizer state, we branch training for a small fixed number of steps under modest perturbations
255   of each task weight.
256   The theory makes two directly testable predictions: checkpoints with larger FitGap should show
257                                     ⋆
      a larger teacher trade-off, and ∥ri,w ∥ should predict how strongly the student changes when wi is
258   increased. We evaluate these predictions using rank correlation and the error of the predicted versus
259   observed per-task KL change. We do not use the local model to claim a long-run global optimum.
260
261   6.2   D OES OPTIMIZER - AWARE SCALE PREDICT TASK PROGRESS ?
262
263   For every sampled task, we log four quantities already available during training: raw gradient norm
264   ∥Gi ∥, lagged-preconditioned norm ∥Pt Gi ∥, realized AdamW parameter-step norm, and next-batch
265   reduction in teacher KL. We compare how well raw and preconditioned norms predict the last two
266
      quantities across tasks and checkpoints. An SGD ablation sets Pt = I; an AdamW-state reset
      ablation tests whether accumulated second moments explain changes in the task ordering.
267
268   This experiment decides which scale OPAS should use. If preconditioned norm does not improve
269   prediction over raw norm, the simpler raw-gradient variant is preferred. Otherwise it directly vali-
      dates the optimizer-aware rule in equation 13.

271
272
      We compare four training rules under the same intended weights, optimizer, student-token budget,
273   and teacher-logit budget: (1) fixed uniform sampling; (2) uncorrected gap-following allocation; (3)
274   OPAS scored by raw gradient norm; and (4) optimizer-aware OPAS scored by ∥Pt Gi ∥. Conditions
275   (3–4) apply wI /aI before the optimizer step; condition (2) intentionally omits it to show when
276   adaptive allocation changes the effective objective.
277
      Efficiency is measured by weighted teacher KL versus tokens, teacher FLOPs, and wall-clock time,
278
      together with tokens required to reach a fixed fraction of the uniform baseline’s total improvement.
279   To test objective preservation, we also report final per-task KL and downstream performance after
280   a matched longer budget. The predicted outcome is specific: OPAS should reduce OptGap faster
281   while approaching the same teacher trade-off as fixed sampling; uncorrected allocation may reach
282   a different trade-off. Optimizer-aware OPAS should outperform raw-norm OPAS only when Sec-
283   tion 6.2 shows that AdamW materially changes task update scales.

286   7     L IMITATIONS
287
288
      Our analysis is deliberately local. We freeze the on-policy prefix distribution within an update and
289
      linearize the student around a checkpoint. During long-horizon training, the prefix distribution,
290
      student Jacobian, teacher–student gap, and optimizer state all evolve. A teacher target that cannot be
291   matched by one local parameter update may become matchable later in training. Our local analysis
292   therefore does not imply that the teachers can never be matched.
293
294
      OPAS minimizes the second moment of a one-step single-task estimator, not wall-clock time or
      long-horizon nonconvex training loss. Its benefit depends on the accuracy and freshness of the
295
      gradient-scale estimates. The sampling floor controls extreme importance ratios but can limit adap-
296
      tation; clipping the ratios themselves would introduce bias. For AdamW, importance correction
297   preserves the conditional expectation of the gradient supplied to the optimizer, but the evolving
298   second-moment state makes the parameter trajectory nonlinear. Our optimizer-aware rule freezes
299   the lagged preconditioner for one sampling decision; it does not claim exact equivalence to a full-
300   mixture AdamW trajectory.

303   8     C ONCLUSION
304
305
306
      Multi-teacher OPD does not simply add independently trained capabilities to a shared model. In the
      usual imperfect-fit regime, training loss combines an irreducible local teacher-fitting gap with un-
307
      finished optimization. Task weights choose the compromise; sampling and the optimizer determine
308
      how quickly training approaches it. OPAS acts on the latter problem: it samples using optimizer-
309   aware update scale and importance-corrects the gradient, reallocating training budget without inten-
310   tionally changing the teacher weights.
