011                                             A BSTRACT
012
013           Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) trains a single student using special-
014           ized teachers, but the effects of loss normalization, vocabulary supervision, and
015           optimizer dynamics on parameter updates and task performance are not well un-
016
              derstood. We study these effects with a Qwen3-1.7B student and four teachers for
              mathematics, code, instruction following, and science. First, assigning equal loss
017
              weights to domains still allows longer responses to receive more weight within
018
              each domain, changing gradient directions. Nevertheless, three normalization
019           rules differ by only 0.14 percentage points in final mean score under top-64 super-
020           vision with Adam. Second, training changes about 97% of FP32 master weights
021           but only 7–8% of stored BF16 weights. Teacher-specific Adam updates appear
022           aligned largely because they use the same accumulated moment estimates: sub-
023           tracting the update that Adam would take with no new gradient reduces their mean
024           cosine similarity from above 0.83 to below 0.01 on domain-specific inputs. Third,
025           top-64 candidate intersections contain over 99.9% of student probability on av-
026           erage in our diagnostics, and expanding supervision to the full vocabulary barely
027
              changes single-step update concentration. Yet top-64 supervision has no consis-
              tent performance advantage over sampled-token supervision: at the joint-training
028
              endpoint, mathematics increases by 2.60 points while GPQA decreases by 1.77
029
              points, with both paired confidence intervals including zero. These results show
030           that sparse stored-weight changes and aligned optimizer updates are insufficient
031           evidence of selective or compatible capability transfer; supervision choices require
032           direct evaluation on each task.

035   1   I NTRODUCTION
036
037   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) is used in LLM post-training to transfer specialized
038   capabilities into a single model (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026; NVIDIA, 2026; Kimi Team et al.,
039   2026; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026). The student generates responses, and a teacher assigned to each
040   prompt domain supplies token-level supervision. The training objective is to improve several capa-
041   bilities while preserving the student’s existing performance. Achieving this requires deciding how
042   much each domain and response contributes to the loss, and how many next-token alternatives the
043   teacher supervises.
044   Recent work establishes both the potential of MOPD and the sensitivity of its results to these
045   choices. Ma et al. (2026) study capability integration with policy-gradient and top-k distillation
046   objectives. Open-MOPD finds uneven transfer between domains and introduces domain token bal-
047   ancing, weighting by student–teacher performance gaps, and refreshed rewards (Gao et al., 2026).
048   The Nemotron 3 Ultra report finds no advantage for top-k or full-vocabulary matching over sampled-
049   token supervision in its preliminary experiments (NVIDIA, 2026, Section 3.3.5). Studies of OPD
050
      parameter changes also report concentration in a small set of stored weights (Yu et al., 2026; Shen
      et al., 2026). These findings leave open how the loss weights and vocabulary targets affect the
051
      gradients, optimizer updates, and task scores of a jointly trained student.
052
053   Three distinctions matter for answering this question. First, equal numbers of prompts per domain
      do not imply equal domain contributions to a token-averaged loss, because response lengths differ.


                                                       1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




054
      Even after fixing each domain’s total loss weight, averaging over tokens or over responses gives dif-
055   ferent weights to long responses. Second, Adam updates depend on accumulated first- and second-
056   moment estimates as well as the current gradient, and small FP32 updates may disappear when
057   weights are stored in BF16. Sparse stored-weight changes or similar Adam update directions can
058   therefore reflect the optimizer and numerical precision. Third, supervising more vocabulary tokens
059   need not improve every task, even when the resulting parameter updates have similar concentration.
060
      We study these distinctions through training runs and controlled gradient and optimizer comparisons
061   with Qwen3-1.7B and four domain teachers. The main findings are:
062
063        1. Loss normalization changes gradients more than final mean scores in our top-
064           64/Adam runs (Section 3). With equal prompt counts, mathematics receives 44.05% of
065           the token-averaged loss weight and instruction following 14.98%. Fixing domain weights
066           does not remove response-length weighting: token and response averaging have a mean
067           gradient cosine of 0.680. Applying the same Adam moment estimates raises their update
068
              cosine to 0.960. The three normalization rules finish within 0.14 mean-score points under
              top-64 supervision, although their learning curves differ; sampled-token/SGD runs have a
069
              larger 1.07-point spread.
070
071        2. BF16 sparsity and Adam update alignment can obscure the current teacher gradient
072
              (Section 4). Joint training changes about 97% of FP32 master weights but only 7–8% of
              stored BF16 weights. At the same student checkpoint, teacher-specific Adam updates have
073
              mean cosine similarity above 0.83 on their respective domain inputs. Removing the step
074
              that Adam would take using its accumulated moments with a zero current gradient reduces
075           the cosine below 0.01. Thus aligned optimizer steps do not establish aligned current teacher
076           gradients.
077
           3. More vocabulary supervision does not consistently improve task performance (Sec-
078           tion 5). Sampled-token, top-64, and full-vocabulary losses produce similarly concentrated
079           single-step updates when the top-64 intersection already contains over 99.9% of student
080           probability on average. In joint training, top-64 supervision scores 2.60 points higher on
081           mathematics and 1.77 points lower on GPQA than sampled-token supervision at update
082           500; all paired task intervals include zero. Its mean advantage also shrinks from 1.69
083           points at update 250 to 0.29 points at update 500.
084
085
      These results motivate evaluating supervision choices with per-task learning curves in addition to
      gradient and parameter statistics. Section 6 examines checkpoint selection on a separate test set:
086
      adding a per-domain retention constraint does not change any selected checkpoint in the ten tested
087
      trajectories.

161   Sampling equal numbers of prompts per domain does not give each domain equal weight in the
      training loss, because student response lengths differ by domain. With token-level normalization

165   equal weight to each response’s mean loss. Domain balancing and within-domain loss normalization

      4.3   R EMOVING THE ZERO - GRADIENT A DAM STEP REVEALS DISTINCT TEACHER
379         DIRECTIONS
380
381   Adam can change parameters even when the current gradient is zero: its first moment retains gradi-
382   ents from earlier updates. To measure how a teacher’s current gradient changes the proposed step,
383   we subtract this zero-gradient baseline. Let U (g; H) be the parameter update computed from gra-
384   dient g using saved state H, which includes the first and second moments and the step count. For
385   teacher d, we compute
386                                      δUd = U (gd ; H) − U (0; H).                                (9)
387   Both steps start from identical parameters and optimizer state. Thus δUd measures the change in
388   the Adam step caused by supplying gd instead of zero. It still depends on the saved moments, and
389   Adam’s nonlinear transformation prevents interpreting it as an additive teacher contribution.
390   With routed inputs, the mean pairwise cosines fall from 0.8774/0.8347 for the complete PG/I64
391   steps to 0.0098/0.0067 for δUd (Figure 3b). With common inputs, they fall from 0.8415/0.8562 to
392   0.2373/0.3462 (panel c). After subtraction, teachers given their respective domain prefixes therefore
393   produce nearly orthogonal update differences, despite the strong alignment of their complete Adam
394   steps. Similar complete steps need not reflect similar directions induced by current teacher gradients.
395   Neither coordinate overlap nor update cosine establishes whether one teacher improves or harms
396   another domain’s performance; that requires per-domain loss or capability evaluation.

491   I64’s mathematics advantage emerges late and does not extend to every task. In single-teacher

497   differences are +2.60 points on mathematics, 0.00 on code, +0.33 on instruction following, and
498   −1.77 on GPQA (Table 1). All four nominal paired 95% intervals include zero, so these point
499   estimates do not establish a consistent task-level improvement (Table 4). The ranking also changes
500   during training. At update 250, I64 scores higher on mathematics, code, and GPQA and ties PG
501   on instruction following; its four-domain mean advantage then shrinks from 1.69 to 0.29 points by

517   6     P RACTICAL I MPLICATIONS AND C HECKPOINT S ELECTION
518
519   The experiments identify two reasons to assess MOPD with per-task evaluations: loss normalization
520   changes the relative contribution of domains and responses, while optimizer moments and weight
521   precision can hide differences between current gradients.
522
523   Choose domain and response weights explicitly. GT gives every token equal weight, so domains
524   with longer responses receive more of the total loss weight. DT fixes each domain’s total weight but
525
      retains the extra weight on longer responses; DR also weights responses equally within each domain.
      Equal prompt quotas do not make these objectives equivalent. The PG/I16/I64 comparisons likewise
526
      show no consistent benefit from increasing the number of supervised vocabulary tokens. Choose this
527
      number using task scores and measured training cost, and report the probability mass covered by the
528   candidate set. Our runs do not support a compute-efficiency ranking because their costs were not
529   matched.
530
531   Separate the current gradient from Adam moments and BF16 rounding. Measure raw gra-
532   dients, FP32 optimizer updates, and BF16 stored-weight changes separately. For teacher compar-
533   isons, start each proposed update from identical student parameters and Adam moment estimates.
534   The zero-gradient reference measures the step driven by the accumulated moments without a new
535   teacher gradient. Its subtraction reveals whether similar complete updates also have similar re-
536   sponses to the current teacher signals. Overlapping changed coordinates or high update cosine alone
537   cannot establish that training with one teacher preserves another task’s performance.
538
539   A retention constraint does not improve checkpoint selection in these runs. We test whether
      ruling out checkpoints with a large task regression improves selection by the mean development

541   points satisfy

      where ϵd ≥ 0 is a prespecified tolerance relative to the initial student. We select the eligible check-

558   7   R ELATED W ORK
559
560   Loss normalization in LLM reinforcement learning. DAPO replaces response-level averaging
561   with token-level averaging to change the contribution of long responses (Yu et al., 2025). Dr. GRPO
562   analyzes the optimization bias introduced by response-length and reward-standard-deviation nor-
563   malization (Liu et al., 2025). Our analysis concerns a distinct choice in multi-domain distillation:
564   fixing each domain’s total loss weight still leaves token-level and response-level averaging within
565   that domain. We measure how this choice changes gradients, Adam updates, and task performance.
566
567   Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD combines specialized teachers through supervision
568   on student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026). Open-MOPD addresses uneven capability transfer
569
      with domain token balancing, performance-gap weighting, and reward refresh (Gao et al., 2026). For
      the same batch and target domain weights, its token-share correction is equivalent to our domain-
570
      token averaging. We further separate this domain correction from response-length weighting within
571
      each domain.
572
573   Parameter changes during post-training. Mukherjee et al. (2025) investigate small parameter
574   subsets that support reinforcement-learning fine-tuning. Yu et al. (2026) find concentrated check-
575   point changes under dense distillation supervision and overlapping changes in students trained sep-
576   arately with different teachers. We instead compare teacher-specific updates from identical copies
577   of a jointly trained student and its Adam state, and distinguish FP32 optimizer updates from BF16
578   stored-weight changes. Shen et al. (2026) study OPD parameter geometry and select response po-
579   sitions for learning. Our vocabulary-supervision comparison varies the candidate tokens scored at
580   each position and evaluates both parameter updates and task scores.

582   8   D ISCUSSION
583
584   The main empirical lesson is that parameter statistics can hide differences that matter for interpreting
585   MOPD. Adam’s accumulated moments make distinct current gradients produce similar updates, and
586   BF16 storage makes dense FP32 parameter changes appear sparse. Neither observation establishes
587   that teachers train disjoint parameter subsets or improve tasks without interference. Conversely,
588   similar single-step concentration under different vocabulary losses does not imply the same task
589   scores after training. In our runs, loss normalization has little effect on the final top-64/Adam mean,
590
      whereas the relative performance of sampled-token and top-k supervision changes with the task and
      checkpoint.
591
592   The scope of these findings is limited to one Qwen3-1.7B student and four teachers fine-tuned from
593   the same initial model. The normalization curves summarize seeds 42–44; the expanded objec-
      tive/optimizer comparison and additional test use seed 42. Repeated response batches measure di-

      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




594
      agnostic sampling variation, while question-bootstrap intervals measure evaluation uncertainty for
595   fixed checkpoints; neither estimates variation between independent training runs. The top-64 inter-
596   sections in the single-step comparisons already retain almost all student probability, so the results
597   do not characterize low-probability vocabulary tails or more dissimilar teachers. Appendix H.4
598   removes four known overlapping mathematics questions as a sensitivity check, rather than an ex-
599   haustive contamination audit. Broader conclusions about training stability or compute efficiency
600   require repeated runs with matched compute budgets.

      The experiments compare averaging rules, teacher-conditioned updates, and vocabulary supervision
813   using a shared student initialization and four domain teachers. The ten joint-training configurations
814   in Table 1 include the original PG-DR and I64-DR/DT/GT branches. Mathematics-only PG/I64
815   runs provide the single-teacher comparison. All checkpoint axes count optimizer updates, and each

818   A.1   M ODEL , DATA , AND TRAINING PROTOCOL
819
820   The student is Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al., 2025). The four frozen teachers are mathematics, code,
821   instruction-following, and science RL descendants of the same initial model. Student and teachers
822
      share the tokenizer and native chat template with thinking disabled. The frozen protocol records
      model revisions and weight identities.
823
824   AdamW configurations use learning rate 2.5 × 10−7 with (β1 , β2 ) = (0.9, 0.98), ϵ = 10−8 , no
825   weight decay or schedule, and global norm-1 clipping. Training sampling uses temperature 1 and
826   top-p = 1, with a 2,048-token prompt cap and 4,096-token response cap. One fresh response is
827   sampled per prompt. Training uses seeds 42–44 for the I64 GT/DT/DR normalization capability
828
      summaries and seed 42 for the other comparisons. Each optimizer update aggregates 64 responses
      across all domain blocks.
829
830   The multi-teacher configurations allocate 16 prompts to each domain per update and use target do-
831   main weights 1/4. Single-teacher configurations allocate all 64 prompts to mathematics. Prompts
832   follow a seeded shuffled schedule, repeated after exhaustion. I64 uses the top-k intersection objec-
833   tive in Equation 4 with k = 64 from initialization; I16 uses k = 16.
835
836   Training prompts come from the Nemotron-3-Nano-RL-Training-Blend: the DAPO and Skywork
837   mathematics subsets, competition coding with unit tests, instruction following, and STEM multiple-
838   choice questions. The frozen derived four-domain release contains the processed prompts and scor-
839   ing metadata. A seeded shuffle reserves separate 64-record diagnostic and held-out splits per do-
840
      main; the remaining training splits contain 21,928 mathematics, 19,041 code, 16,447 instruction-
      following, and 19,542 science records.

844   Each capability suite contains MATH-500 (500 questions from the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks
845   et al., 2021); dataset release), LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2024),
846   IFBench strict (300 questions) (Pyatkin et al., 2025), and GPQA Diamond (198 questions) (Rein
847   et al., 2023). Mathematics, code, and instruction following use one greedy response per question.
848   GPQA uses four responses per question at temperature 1 and top-p = 1. Its score is the mean
849   correctness over all four responses, then over questions: average@4 accuracy. Evaluation permits
850   up to 32,768 response tokens, bounded by the remaining configured 32,768-token context. Each
851   complete suite contains 1,720 responses.

853   100/250/500 for three SGD trajectories, plus the shared initial evaluation. Appendix H.1 describes

1233   With domain losses averaged separately, prompt quotas and loss weights play different roles: in-
1234   creasing a quota at fixed λd changes exposure and estimation variance without directly increasing
1235   that domain’s expected gradient weight. The single-teacher runs receive four times as many mathe-

1237   to teacher count alone.

1240   h)/pθ (S | h). At a fixed prefix with a nonempty intersection and detached coefficients, the I64
1241   gradient is r(h) times the gradient obtained by renormalizing the same weights over I. Thus re-
       moving length weighting does not remove variation in feedback scale across prefixes. Monitoring
