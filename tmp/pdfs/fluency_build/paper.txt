009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation transfers domain expertise into a shared stu-
012            dent. We study how loss averaging, optimizer history, and vocabulary super-
013            vision shape this transfer, using Qwen3-1.7B and four domain specialists, with
014            additional SmolLM3-3B measurements. Token and response averaging produce
015            different gradients whose update directions become more similar after applying
016            saved Adam moments; final mean scores are close. Training changes most pa-
017
               rameters, although low-precision storage hides many of these changes. Adam’s
               accumulated moments also account for much of the alignment between teacher-
018
               specific updates: subtracting the zero-gradient step reduces their mean cosine
019
               from above 0.83 to below 0.01 on domain-specific inputs. Expanding supervi-
020            sion from a sampled token to the top-64 intersection improves final mathematics
021            accuracy and lowers science accuracy, with relative gains changing during train-
022            ing. Top-64 closely approximates the full-vocabulary gradient direction in Qwen,
023            while SmolLM retains appreciable gradient error. Together, these findings con-
024            nect local update measurements to task-dependent learning trajectories and iden-
025            tify optimizer history and numerical precision as central to interpreting distillation
026            updates.

      1   I NTRODUCTION
030
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) transfers specialized capabilities into a single model
031
      (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026; NVIDIA, 2026; Kimi Team et al., 2026; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026).
032
      The student generates responses, and a teacher assigned to each prompt domain supplies token-level
033   supervision. Improving several capabilities requires deciding how much each domain and response
034   contributes to the loss, and how many next-token alternatives the teacher supervises.
035
036
      Recent work establishes both the potential of MOPD and its sensitivity to these choices. Ma et al.
      (2026) study capability integration with policy-gradient and top-k objectives. Open-MOPD ad-
037
      dresses uneven transfer through domain token balancing, performance-gap weighting, and refreshed
038
      rewards (Gao et al., 2026). The Nemotron 3 Ultra report finds no advantage for top-k or full-
039   vocabulary matching over sampled-token supervision in preliminary experiments (NVIDIA, 2026,
040   Section 3.3.5). Other studies report parameter changes concentrated in a small set of stored weights
041   (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). How these observations connect loss construction, optimizer
042   updates, and task performance remains open.
043
      Our study follows the path from loss construction to gradients, parameter updates, and task perfor-
044
      mance. The main experiments train Qwen3-1.7B with teachers for mathematics, code, instruction
045   following, and science. We vary loss averaging and vocabulary supervision during training, and
046   compare gradients and optimizer steps. Three findings emerge:
047
048        1. Adam brings different normalization gradients closer in update direction (Section 3).
049           Weighting responses by length or equally produces gradients with mean cosine 0.68; Adam
050           raises the update cosine to 0.96. The averaging rules reach similar final mean scores and
051           follow different task-level learning curves.
052        2. Precision and optimizer history strongly shape measured update sparsity and align-
053           ment (Section 4). Training changes most FP32 weights, while BF16 rounding leaves a
              small fraction visibly changed. Teacher-specific steps are strongly aligned through shared


                                                         1

054
               Adam moments; subtracting the zero-gradient step leaves near-zero mean alignment on
055            domain-specific inputs.
056
            3. Broader vocabulary supervision produces task-dependent gains (Section 5). Top-64
057
               supervision improves final mathematics accuracy and lowers science accuracy relative to
058
               sampled-token supervision. Its mean advantage narrows as training proceeds. The losses
059            produce similar update concentration.
060
061   Additional SmolLM3-3B measurements show that top-64 retains high average student probability
062   while omitting substantial mass on some inputs. Enlarging the candidate set improves gradient
063   approximation, with residual error relative to full-vocabulary supervision. Section 6 connects these
064   observations to checkpoint selection: individual tasks can regress during training and recover by
065   the endpoint, and development-set rankings can change on separate test questions. These results
066
      motivate tracking per-task learning curves alongside gradients and parameter changes.

      3.2                                  T RAINED A DAM MOMENTS ALIGN UPDATES FROM DIFFERENT AVERAGING RULES
195
196
197   Different gradients produce closely aligned Adam steps. Equal response weighting and within-
198   domain token weighting produce gradients with mean cosine 0.68. Adam raises their update cosine
199
      to 0.96 (Figure 1b). Comparisons with global token averaging show the same pattern.
200   Adam transforms the current gradient through its accumulated first and second moments (Kingma
201   & Ba, 2015).
202
      In SmolLM, zero-initialized Adam moments lower the response-versus-token cosine from 0.59–
203   0.90 for raw gradients to 0.41–0.70 for steps. Together, these observations make the optimizer state
204   central to interpreting update alignment.
205
206
207
208   Clipping preserves gradient direction; optimizer history shapes the step. Equal response
209   weighting triggers clipping throughout training, and token weighting triggers it less often (Fig-
210   ure 2a). Global norm clipping preserves pairwise gradient cosines. Resetting Adam’s moments
211   changes step norms and held-out loss changes, while norm-matched SGD steps separate the aver-
212
      aging rules more strongly (panels b,c). These interventions show how accumulated moments shape
      the effect of the current gradient.
213
214   One-step improvements also vary by domain. For example, global token averaging changes mean
215   full-vocabulary KL by −0.027 × 10−3 and instruction-following KL by +0.272 × 10−3 . The ag-
      gregate improvement coexists with a domain-level regression.

250   We examine numerical precision and shared optimizer state when interpreting concentrated check-
251   point changes (Yu et al., 2026) and teacher-update alignment.

253   4.1                   BF16 ROUNDING HIDES WIDESPREAD FP32 CHANGES
254
255   Joint training changes about 97% of FP32 master weights. After BF16 rounding, only 7–9% of
256
      weights differ visibly from initialization (Figure 3a). Small updates are widespread in FP32 and
      disappear when the weights are stored in BF16.
257
258   The precision effect also extends to the concentration of update magnitude. About one third of FP32
259   coordinates carry 90% of squared cumulative change, compared with about 4% of BF16 coordinates.
260   Loss averaging provides a further comparison between parameter changes and performance. The
261   averaging rules produce different fractions of changed BF16 weights while reaching similar final
262   mean scores.
263
264   4.2                   S HARED A DAM MOMENTS ALIGN TEACHER - SPECIFIC UPDATES
265
266   Teacher-specific steps are strongly aligned by Adam. On responses from each teacher’s own domain,
267   mean update cosines exceed 0.83 under both sampled-token and top-64 supervision. Resetting the
268   first moment while retaining the second, or replacing Adam with norm-matched SGD, reduces the
269   mean cosine to near zero (Figure 3b). The accumulated first moment accounts for much of the
      alignment.

      5.2   L ARGER CANDIDATE SETS IMPROVE GRADIENT APPROXIMATION
344
345   Truncating vocabulary support omits probability mass and changes token weights (Anshumann et al.,
346   2025; Huang & Wei, 2026). We measureP   coverage as the student probability retained by the student–
347   teacher candidate intersection, ck (h) = v∈S∩T pθ (v | h), and compare gradients.
348
349   Top-64 closely matches the full-vocabulary direction in Qwen. The intersection retains over
350   99.9% of student probability on average, and its gradient direction is close to that of full-vocabulary
351   supervision (Figure 4b). Averaging 1, 16, and 64 token samples raises the mean gradient cosine
352   from 0.54 to 0.87 to 0.97; top-64 reaches 0.99999 (Figure 6a). This sampling control identifies
353   finite-sample variation as one source of the directional difference.
354
355   Lower-coverage inputs retain appreciable gradient error in SmolLM. SmolLM3-3B measure-
356   ments use the MixSFT initialization and its student after 500 updates of top-64 training with response
357
      averaging and three teachers for mathematics, code, and instruction following (Gao et al., 2026).
      Mean top-64 coverage is 97.42% initially and 98.56% after training; 8.42% and 3.65% of weighted
358
      prefixes retain less than 90% mass (Figure 5a,b). Larger candidate sets reduce this low-coverage
359
      tail.
360
361   We compare gradients on typical prefixes, high-entropy prefixes, and prefixes with top-64 coverage
362   below 99%. Top-64 has 2.16–24.54% relative gradient error against full-vocabulary supervision.
363
      Top-4 and top-8 produce larger errors in the evaluated batches (Figure 5d). Here relative error is
      the norm of the gradient difference divided by the full-vocabulary gradient norm, capturing both
364
      direction and magnitude. Larger candidate sets improve the approximation, with residual error at
365
      top-64.
366
367
      SmolLM capability scores also reveal changes below the aggregate. Sampled-token training

      7   R ELATED W ORK
456
      Distillation objectives and vocabulary truncation. MiniLLM uses reverse KL (Gu et al., 2024);
457
      Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD) combines student-generated sequences with alterna-
458   tive divergences (Agarwal et al., 2024); f -DISTILL derives word-level losses from sequence-level
459   f -divergences (Wen et al., 2023). Sparse Logit Sampling analyzes top-k caching bias (Anshu-
460   mann et al., 2025), while Tail-Aware OPD restores tail-mass information lost by top-k renormal-
461   ization (Huang & Wei, 2026). Our intersection loss retains full-softmax log ratios with detached
462   student-top-k weights. Section 5 compares its coverage, gradient error, updates, and task scores
463   with sampled-token and full-vocabulary supervision.
464
465   Multi-teacher distillation and loss normalization. MOPD integrates specialized teachers on
466   student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026); Open-MOPD adds domain token balancing and adap-
467
      tive weighting (Gao et al., 2026). DAPO uses token-level averaging (Yu et al., 2025), and Dr. GRPO
      analyzes length and reward-normalization bias (Liu et al., 2025). We separate domain weighting
468
      from within-domain response weighting: Open-MOPD’s token-share correction gives DT for fixed
469
      batches and domain weights, while DR also removes response-length weighting.
470
471
      Post-training parameter geometry. Mukherjee et al. (2025) study small parameter subsets sup-
472   porting RL fine-tuning; Yu et al. (2026) report concentrated distillation updates and overlap between
473   separately trained students. Shen et al. (2026) examine OPD geometry and response-position selec-
474   tion. We examine teacher alignment, distinguish FP32 from BF16 changes, and vary vocabulary
475   support.

      8   D ISCUSSION
478
479
      The path from a distillation loss to task performance passes through optimizer history, numeri-
480   cal precision, and an evolving training trajectory. In our comparisons, Adam aligns directionally
481   different gradients, and BF16 rounding hides widespread FP32 changes. Broader vocabulary su-
482   pervision improves gradient approximation and produces task-dependent learning gains, including
483   a mathematics–science trade-off and changing advantages over time. These findings give comple-
484   mentary roles to the measurements: gradients describe the immediate training signal, parameter
485   changes show its interaction with the optimizer and precision, and per-task learning curves reveal
      the resulting capability gains and regressions.

      A.1   M ODEL , DATA , AND TRAINING PROTOCOL
780
781   The main-study student is Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al., 2025); training uses seeds 42, 43, and 44.
782   The four teachers were trained by reinforcement learning from the same initial model, specializing
783   in mathematics, code, instruction following, and science, respectively. Student and teachers share
784   the tokenizer and native chat template with thinking disabled. The experiment records identify the
785   model revisions and weight files.
786   AdamW configurations use learning rate 2.5 × 10−7 with (β1 , β2 ) = (0.9, 0.98), ϵ = 10−8 , no
787   weight decay or schedule, and global norm-1 clipping. Training sampling uses temperature 1 and
788   top-p = 1, with a 2,048-token prompt cap and 4,096-token response cap. One fresh response is
789   sampled per prompt. Each optimizer update aggregates 64 responses; domain allocation is specified
790   below.
791
      The multi-teacher setup allocates 16 prompts to each domain per update and uses target domain
792   weights 1/4. The single-teacher setup allocates all 64 prompts to the same domain. I64 uses the
793   top-64 objective in Equation 3; I16 uses k = 16. Comparisons within each optimizer share initializa-
794   tion, teachers, and prompt schedules. The vocabulary-supervision comparison also holds response
795   averaging and optimizer settings fixed within each training setting.

1123   When each domain loss is averaged separately, its prompt quota and loss weight control different
1124   quantities. Increasing the quota at fixed λd changes the number of sampled responses and the vari-

1127   as joint runs.
1128   Candidate-set coverage also scales the distillation gradient. For the student candidate set S and
1129   its intersection I with the teacher set in Equation 3, the retained student weights sum to r(h) =
1130   pθ (I | h)/pθ (S | h). At a fixed prefix with a nonempty intersection and detached coefficients,
1131   the I64 gradient equals r(h) times the gradient obtained by renormalizing the same weights over
1132   I. Coverage therefore changes gradient scale across prefixes even under equal response weighting.
1133   Measuring the retained mass and empty-intersection rate by domain can reveal unequal coverage
       under a fixed candidate size.

1493   Within each domain we select representative prefixes, prefixes above its 75th entropy percentile, and
1494   prefixes with I64 mass below 0.99. Selection allows overlap between strata and uses up to 32 distinct

1496   prefixes overall. Each stratum is divided into three batches. Small candidate budgets k = 4, 8 are
1497   selected by a coverage-only rule.
1498   The BF16 coverage audit sorts a top-128 list and takes its first k entries, whereas gradient diagnostics
1499   recompute top-k directly in FP32 at the exported BF16 parameter values. Student forward/backward
1500   passes use FP32; cached teacher targets use BF16 forward passes followed by FP32 log-softmax.
1501   One of the 85 frozen low-coverage prefixes crosses 0.99 during the FP32 diagnostic and remains in
1502   its original stratum. The FP32 diagnostic mean I64 masses are 99.049%, 95.684%, and 93.121% for
1503   the representative, high-entropy, and low-coverage groups.
1504   Full KL, PG, and intersection objectives use the definitions in Appendix D. Empty intersections
1505   produce zero loss. We retain zero- and small-norm cases and report vector differences in addition
1506   to cosine. The plotted relative error is ∥gIk − gfull ∥/∥gfull ∥, which measures the vector difference
