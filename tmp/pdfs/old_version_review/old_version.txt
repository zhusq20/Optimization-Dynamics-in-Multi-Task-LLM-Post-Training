009                                               A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) consolidates reinforcement-learned
012            specialists into one model, but its finite-budget dynamics are often attributed
013            to cross-task gradient interference. We show that, under exact teachers ob-
014            tained from a shared KL reference, ideal reverse-KL MOPD is the correspond-
015            ing KL-regularized multi-task RL objective up to known task-wise scales. The
016            two methods therefore share stationary points and gradient-conflict angles after
017
               temperature-aware task weighting. Their raw interference vanishes only when
               every task field vanishes; aggregate stationarity or a shared initialization is insuf-
018
               ficient. We then analyze the practical frozen-prefix token field directly. Its path
019
               is determined by effective task/token exposure, action-centered teacher signal, re-
020            ward scale and temperature, teacher approximation, stochastic covariance, local
021            curvature, staleness, and the optimizer. A local dynamics model shows that task
022            ratios can produce arbitrarily different convergence rates even when pairwise in-
023            terference is identically zero, and yields a closed-form minimum-time allocation
024            in a separable regime. Thus interference is a useful downstream diagnostic, but
025            not a sufficient explanation of MOPD optimization.

028   1   I NTRODUCTION
029
030   Post-training a single language model across mathematics, coding, instruction following, and tool
031
      use creates an allocation problem: tasks differ in reward sparsity, response length, learning speed,
      and optimization noise. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) addresses this problem by first
032
      training task specialists with RL and then distilling their token distributions on student-generated
033
      trajectories (Ma et al., 2026). The pipeline can integrate capabilities, yet some tasks improve much
034   earlier than others under a fixed compute budget.
035
036   Cross-task gradient interference is a natural explanation. For task fields gi and gj , a negative inner
037
      product means that an infinitesimal update for one objective locally hurts the other. Recent analy-
      ses contrast the gradient geometry of supervised and reinforcement learning (Zhu et al., 2026) and
038
      document gradient imbalance in multi-task RL post-training (Wu et al., 2025). However, an inner
039
      product is computed only after the data mixture, teacher signal, reward scale, token reduction, cur-
040   vature, noise, and optimizer have formed the update. It cannot identify which of these upstream
041   variables caused a trajectory. Moreover, raw interference approaches zero whenever both gradient
042   norms shrink, even if their cosine remains −1.
043
      Our starting point is an exact identity. If specialist i is the Boltzmann optimum of a KL-regularized
044
      RL objective with reference π0 and temperature βi , then ideal reverse-KL distillation toward that
045
      specialist has exactly the RL task field scaled by 1/βi . Consequently, ideal MOPD does not possess
046   a different sign or angle of gradient conflict. A shared base checkpoint alone is not sufficient: the
047   identity requires the shared KL reference, the intended reward and temperature, and an exact teacher.
048   This separates the destination from the path. The destination follows from the ideal objective; the
049   practical path is formed by the realized token field and its finite-budget implementation.
050
      This distinction is important because concurrent work, including Open-MOPD, already identifies
051
      response-length imbalance, convergence drift, and stale student rewards as empirical bottlenecks.1

079   Our goal is complementary: to give a compact theory that explains why such variables matter even
080   when gradient interference is absent, and to connect them to teacher temperature, local score/Fisher
081   geometry, stochastic covariance, and minimum-time task allocation.
082   We make three contributions. First, we prove exact objective, gradient, interference, and
083   temperature-aware mixture relations between ideal MOPD and KL-regularized multi-task RL, to-
084   gether with the conditional scope of interference extinction. Second, taking the practical frozen-
085   prefix token update as the object of study, we derive a centered-signal path law that exposes effective
086   token allocation, reward/teacher quality, covariance, curvature, and optimizer state. Third, we de-
087   rive a local convergence model and a closed-form finite-budget allocation rule, then specify targeted
088
      experiments that distinguish this theory from explanations based only on gradient conflict.

090   2        R ELATED W ORK
091
092   On-policy and multi-teacher distillation. On-policy distillation queries a teacher on student-
093   generated states, reducing the train–test state mismatch of offline imitation. MOPD extends this
094   idea to capability integration by routing each domain to an independently trained RL specialist (Ma
095   et al., 2026). Our analysis focuses on the resulting multi-task optimization field: when the ideal
096
      teacher construction preserves the RL objective, and which practical quantities still determine finite-
      time behavior.
097
098
      Multi-task gradient geometry. Multi-objective learning commonly diagnoses conflict through
099
      task-gradient inner products and modifies updates using adaptive weighting or gradient
100   surgery (Sener & Koltun, 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024). In LLM post-training, recent
101   work studies gradient imbalance in multi-task RL (Wu et al., 2025), proposes task-aware GRPO
102   variants (Ramesh et al., 2026), and argues that centered RL updates can exhibit weaker raw cross-
103   task interaction than supervised updates (Zhu et al., 2026). We retain interference as a local signed
104   diagnostic, but distinguish raw inner-product contraction from angular orthogonality and from con-
105   vergence of the aggregate objective.
106
107   Optimization-budget imbalance. Open-MOPD empirically attributes capability imbalance to un-
      equal token budgets, heterogeneous convergence, and stale student-dependent signals. Our theory

231   4    E MPIRICAL T ESTS
232
233   We organize the empirical evaluation around four causal tests. Each test specifies a prediction that
234
      can be falsified without relying on a global ranking of final task performance.
235
236
      Test 1: exact-teacher equivalence. Use a finite response space or a small language model for
      which Zi (x) and exact Boltzmann teachers can be evaluated. For random checkpoints, compare giM
237
      with giRL /βi in relative norm and cosine. Then vary βi and compare equal teacher sampling with
238
      the calibrated mixture in Eq. (5). Only the calibrated mixture should reproduce the target RL field
239
      up to a global time scale. A shared initialization with an undertrained or reward-mismatched teacher
240   serves as the negative control.
241
242   Test 2: same endpoint, different paths. Construct routed tasks with a common realizable end-
243   point and hold the total number of valid optimization tokens, learning rate, and optimizer fixed.
244   Sweep configured prompt ratios, response-length distributions, and loss reductions to create differ-
245   ent realized qieff . At each checkpoint, log configured prompt share, valid-token share, post-weighting
246   share, per-task loss, centered signal radius from Eq. (10), gradient norm, raw interference, and co-
247   sine. The key prediction is that changing qieff changes per-task convergence rates even when the
248   interference matrix is unchanged or identically zero. Plotting progress against cumulative effective
249
      tokens should collapse curves better than plotting against global steps.
250
251
      Test 3: reward, teacher, and realization quality. Independently vary reward scale or teacher
      temperature, teacher training budget, rollout freshness, and sampled-token versus full-vocabulary or
252
      truncated updates. For teacher quality, estimate the action-centered residual in Eq. (13) on student-
253
      visited prefixes rather than using only the teacher’s final task score. For stochastic realization, report
254   per-task covariance or signal-to-noise ratio under equal compute. For staleness, compare one fresh
255   update, repeated updates with stale student-dependent quantities, and repeated updates after refresh-
256   ing those quantities. The theory predicts that these interventions can alter the path while leaving
257   ideal task-gradient angles unchanged.
258
259   Allocation test. Fit κi λi from short single-task or lightly mixed learning curves, compute Eq. (17),
260   and compare its static mixture against equal prompts, equal valid tokens, and a conflict-minimizing
261   scheduler under a matched token budget. Evaluation should report time to reach all prespecified
262   task thresholds, final per-task performance, and uncertainty across seeds. This directly tests whether
263
      remaining headroom and learnability predict allocation better than raw gradient conflict.

265   5    D ESIGN I MPLICATIONS
266
267   The analysis yields three practical principles. First, distinguish objective weights from data fre-
268   quency. If teachers use different KL temperatures, equal teacher sampling does not represent an
269   equal-weight RL objective; task weights should first be calibrated by Eq. (5), and sampling may
      then be changed with appropriate reweighting to control estimator variance.


                                                          5
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




270
      Second, allocate optimization budget using realized exposure and remaining demand. Prompt counts
271   are insufficient when response lengths, masking, and loss reductions differ. A scheduler should
272   monitor effective valid-token share, residual teacher signal, and a curvature or empirical learning-
273   rate proxy. Equation (17) suggests allocating more budget to tasks with larger remaining log gap or
274   lower usable signal and curvature, rather than optimizing pairwise cosine alone.
275
      Third, monitor the field that is actually implemented. Teacher benchmark quality, full-vocabulary
276
      KL, and prompt ratios can all look favorable while the student receives a weak action-centered signal
277   on visited prefixes. Logging centered teacher residuals, token-level covariance, truncation mass, and
278   rollout-to-current KL separates teacher error, estimator noise, and staleness. Gradient interference
279   remains useful as a warning that the resulting fields oppose one another, but reducing it is not a
280   substitute for correcting the upstream allocation or signal.

      6   D ISCUSSION AND L IMITATIONS
283
284   Our claim is deliberately narrower than “MOPD and RL always converge to zero interference from
285   the same base model.” Exact equivalence requires a common KL reference and teachers that are
286   exact optima for the stated rewards and temperatures. Conditional interference extinction further
287   requires a common task-wise stationary point. A joint stationary point can instead contain nonzero
288   opposing task fields, and a vanishing raw inner product need not imply a vanishing cosine.
289
      The practical path law is local. Neural-network nonconvexity, changing rollout coverage, adaptive
290   optimizers, and finite-step clipping can depart from the quadratic and small-step approximations.
291   Teacher incompatibility or limited student capacity can also make the endpoint depend on the task
292   mixture. The minimum-time allocation is therefore a testable local prescription, not a global guar-
293   antee.
294
      Finally, low gradient conflict is neither necessary nor sufficient for end-to-end performance. A task
295
      can receive too little effective budget while remaining orthogonal to every other task, and two tasks
296   can have negative local interaction while a shared representation improves final generalization. The
297   intended use of interference is diagnostic: report it together with effective exposure, signal mag-
298   nitude, curvature, covariance, and teacher mismatch rather than treating it as a standalone success
299   criterion.
