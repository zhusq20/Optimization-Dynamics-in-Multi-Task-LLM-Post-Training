011                                                A BSTRACT
012
013            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) is increasingly used to integrate in-
014            dependently post-trained specialists into one language model. Hard routing sepa-
015            rates the source of supervision, but not the effects of training: changing one task’s
016
               loss weight can affect capabilities outside that task, and an update for one task
               can change predictions on another even when their parameter gradients are nearly
017
               orthogonal.
018
               We study a simple question: when can one student fit all routed teachers at the
019
               same time? Around a student checkpoint, we freeze the on-policy prefixes used
020
               for distillation and linearize the student logits. Under the standard local quadratic
021            approximation to reverse KL, MOPD becomes a weighted least-squares problem
022            between the teacher–student logit gaps and the logit changes induced by a shared
023            parameter update.
024            This view gives two regimes. If one parameter update can match every teacher’s
025            logit gap, all positive task weights yield the same fitted logits, although training
026            rates can differ. Otherwise, the weights determine how the unavoidable local fit-
027            ting error is distributed across teachers. Based on this distinction, we introduce
028            Objective-Preserving Adaptive Sampling (OPAS): it samples tasks in proportion
029            to their weighted gradient scale and importance-corrects each update. Its ideal al-
030
               location minimizes the second moment of the single-task gradient estimator while
               preserving the intended task objective, so training budget can be reallocated with-
031
               out silently changing the teacher trade-off.

034   1   I NTRODUCTION
035
036   Large language models are increasingly post-trained with specialized objectives for reasoning, cod-
037   ing, instruction following, and other capabilities. A natural way to combine independently trained
038   specialists is multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD): prompts from each domain are routed to
039   the corresponding teacher, while a single student is trained to match all teachers.
040
      At first glance, hard routing appears to separate the tasks. Each example is supervised by only
041
      one teacher, so one might expect independently trained capabilities to be transferred independently.
042   Recent observations suggest otherwise. Multi-task RL can exhibit weak or nearly orthogonal task
043   gradients (Zhu et al., 2026), while MOPD can still show strong cross-domain effects when teacher
044   mixtures are changed (Li et al., 2026). Other work finds substantial imbalance even under oracle
045   routing, with response length and optimization exposure playing an important role (Gao et al., 2026).
046
      These phenomena are difficult to reconcile if specialist integration is viewed only through pairwise
047
      gradient conflict. We instead ask a more basic question:
048
049            Can the student simultaneously learn the capabilities of all teachers?
050
051   This question separates two qualitatively different situations. Suppose the student can fit all teacher
052   targets at once. Then changing the task loss weights does not change the target predictions on the
053   supervised data; it mainly changes which tasks are learned faster. In contrast, if the teacher targets
      cannot be fit simultaneously, the task weights determine how the student trades off their fitting errors.

      This distinction also suggests an objective-preserving training intervention. Let wi denote the in-
077
      tended task loss weight and ai the probability of sampling task i. Increasing wi changes the target
078   objective and, for incompatible teachers, can change the local optimum. Increasing ai while rescal-
079   ing the sampled update by wi /ai instead changes where training steps are spent without changing
080   the expected objective. We use this separation to derive Objective-Preserving Adaptive Sampling
081   (OPAS), whose ideal allocation samples tasks according to their weighted gradient scale and mini-
082   mizes the second moment of the resulting unbiased gradient estimator.
083
      Our contributions are therefore threefold. First, we formulate MOPD as the problem of fitting mul-
084   tiple routed teacher policies with one shared student and identify when teacher weights can change
085   the locally optimal predictions. Second, we show that hard routing and gradient orthogonality do
086   not guarantee isolation of teacher effects across tasks. Third, we turn the distinction between task
087   weighting and sampling into OPAS, a practical rule for reallocating training budget without chang-
088   ing the intended task weights.

      2   R ELATED W ORK
092
093   Multi-teacher distillation and capability integration. Open-MOPD attributes capability imbal-
094
      ance to unequal token counts, teacher–student gaps, and stale rewards, and uses these signals to
      rebalance training (Gao et al., 2026). Complementary work shows that changing teacher data pro-
095
      portions can produce cross-domain “seesaw” effects even with oracle routing (Li et al., 2026). These
096
      results motivate our distinction between task loss weights and task sampling probabilities. OPAS
097   may use the same gap signal to decide where to spend training steps, but importance-corrects the
098   sampled update so that the intended task objective remains fixed. Our compatibility analysis ex-
099   plains why this distinction is especially important when one student cannot match all teacher targets
100   simultaneously.
101
102   Multi-task update geometry. Recent work finds that multi-task RL gradients can be substantially
103   more orthogonal than SFT gradients and uses this observation to motivate independent RL training
104   followed by parameter merging (Zhu et al., 2026). We study a complementary question: whether
105   a task update changes another task’s predictions. Parameter-gradient orthogonality constrains one
106   scalar inner product, whereas the cross-task Jacobian response Ji gj measures the full first-order
107   change in task-i logits. We therefore retain the local least-squares and Jacobian analyses as diagnos-
      tics, and use only the separation between weighting and sampling to derive a training method.

373   8     L IMITATIONS
374
375   Our analysis is deliberately local. We freeze the on-policy prefix distribution within an update and
376   linearize the student around a checkpoint. During long-horizon training, the prefix distribution,
377   student Jacobian, teacher–student gap, and optimizer state all evolve. A teacher target that cannot be
      matched by one local parameter update may become matchable later in training. Our local analysis


                                                          7
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




378
      therefore does not imply that the teachers can never be matched. The KL-RL connection is also
379   exact only for the fixed-reference sequence-level objective in equation 5; practical PPO, GRPO,
380   clipping, and frozen-prefix OPD need not follow identical dynamics.
381
382
      OPAS minimizes the second moment of a one-step single-task estimator, not wall-clock time or long-
      horizon nonconvex training loss. Its benefit depends on the accuracy and freshness of the gradient-
383
      scale estimates. The sampling floor controls extreme importance ratios but can limit adaptation;
384
      clipping the ratios themselves would introduce bias. Output-aware interference control based on
385   ∥Ji u∥ is a promising extension, but requires additional Jacobian–vector products and may suppress
386   positive transfer.

389   9   C ONCLUSION
390
391   Multi-teacher OPD does not simply add independently trained capabilities to a shared model. It asks
392   one student to fit several routed teacher policies at the same time. The key distinction is whether one
393   local parameter update can match all teacher–student logit gaps. When it can, task weights mainly
394
      affect training speed; when it cannot, the weights determine the local trade-off between incompat-
      ible teacher targets. Shared parameters also imply that hard routing and gradient orthogonality are
395
      insufficient for task isolation. OPAS acts on the complementary problem of training imbalance: it
396
      adapts task sampling while importance-correcting each update, reallocating training budget without
397   changing the intended task weights.
