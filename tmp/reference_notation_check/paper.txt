009                                              A BSTRACT
010
011            Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) combines domain specialists into a
012            single student, but how loss normalization, optimizer dynamics, and vocabulary
013            supervision shape its updates and task performance remains unclear. We study
014            these choices with Qwen3-1.7B and four reinforcement-learned teachers. First,
015            token- and response-level averaging produce distinct gradients but similar final
016            scores under top-64 supervision and Adam; the spread is larger under sampled-
017            token supervision with SGD, while sampled-token Adam endpoints are nearly
018
               tied. Second, joint training changes about 97% of FP32 parameters, whereas
               only 7.3–8.5% differ after BF16 rounding. The apparent alignment of teacher-
019
               specific updates largely reflects Adam’s accumulated moments: subtracting the
020
               zero-gradient step reduces their mean cosine from above 0.83 to below 0.01 on
021            domain-specific inputs. Third, the top-64 intersection retains over 99.9% of stu-
022            dent probability on average at the evaluated Qwen checkpoints, yet provides no
023            consistent task advantage over sampled-token supervision. SmolLM3-3B diag-
024            nostics further reveal low-coverage tails and appreciable gradient error despite
025            high mean coverage and cosine. These results distinguish current gradients from
026            optimizer history and stored-weight changes, and show why vocabulary super-
027            vision should be assessed through per-task learning curves alongside parameter
028            statistics.

      1   I NTRODUCTION
032
033
      Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) transfers specialized capabilities into a single model
      (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026; NVIDIA, 2026; Kimi Team et al., 2026; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026).
034
      The student generates responses, and a teacher assigned to each prompt domain supplies token-level
035
      supervision. Improving several capabilities requires deciding how much each domain and response
036   contributes to the loss, and how many next-token alternatives the teacher supervises.
037
038   Recent work establishes both the potential of MOPD and its sensitivity to these choices. Ma et al.
039
      (2026) study capability integration with policy-gradient and top-k objectives. Open-MOPD ad-
      dresses uneven transfer through domain token balancing, performance-gap weighting, and refreshed
040
      rewards (Gao et al., 2026). The Nemotron 3 Ultra report finds no advantage for top-k or full-
041
      vocabulary matching over sampled-token supervision in preliminary experiments (NVIDIA, 2026,
042   Section 3.3.5). Other studies report parameter changes concentrated in a small set of stored weights
043   (Yu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). How these observations connect loss construction, optimizer
044   updates, and task performance remains open.
045
      We distinguish three effects. Equal prompt counts do not imply equal domain loss weights when
046
      response lengths differ; fixing domain weights still leaves a choice between token and response
047
      averaging. Adam mixes the current gradient with accumulated moments, while BF16 storage can
048   hide small FP32 changes. Finally, supervising more vocabulary tokens need not improve every task.
049   We examine these effects with Qwen3-1.7B and four domain teachers:
050
051        1. Normalization changes gradients more than final top-64/Adam scores (Section 3).
052           Domain-token and domain-response gradients have mean cosine 0.680, rising to 0.960 af-
053           ter applying the same Adam state. The three averaging rules finish within 0.17 mean-score
              points, although their learning curves differ.

054
            2. BF16 sparsity and Adam alignment obscure current teacher signals (Section 4). Joint
055            training changes about 97% of FP32 weights but only 7.3–8.5% of BF16 weights. Subtract-
056            ing Adam’s zero-gradient step reduces the mean teacher-update cosine from above 0.83 to
057            below 0.01 on domain-specific inputs.
058
            3. More vocabulary supervision does not consistently improve task performance (Sec-
059            tion 5). Top-64 and full-vocabulary supervision yield similar single-step concentration.
060            Against sampled-token supervision, top-64’s joint-training mean advantage shrinks from
061            1.69 points at update 250 to 0.43 at update 500, with mixed per-task differences.
062
063   SmolLM3-3B diagnostics extend the vocabulary comparison to lower-coverage conditions: high
064   average intersection mass can conceal a low-coverage tail, and high gradient cosine can coexist with
065
      appreciable vector error. Section 6 connects these measurements to checkpoint selection: a per-
      domain retention constraint leaves all ten development-selected checkpoints unchanged. Together,
066
      the results motivate per-task learning curves alongside gradient and parameter statistics.

126   3.2   S IMILAR I64 ENDPOINT SCORES DESPITE DIFFERENT LOSS WEIGHTS
127
      Experimental setting. We train Qwen3-1.7B for 500 updates with four RL-specialized teachers
128
      derived from the same base model for mathematics, code, instruction following, and science. Each
129
      update uses 16 fresh responses per domain. The I64 GT/DT/DR runs share initialization, teachers,

136   tion following each receive 25% of prompts but account for 44.05% and 14.98% of GT’s valid
137   response tokens in the first 50 batches. DT and DR fix both loss weights at 25% (Figure 1a). Yet

      age points. The preferred rule varies by task and checkpoint: all three approach teacher performance

      (Appendix A.3); the close point estimates do not establish equivalence.

150   3.3   A DAM REDUCES DIRECTIONAL DIFFERENCES BETWEEN NORMALIZATION RULES
151
152
      Adam’s adaptive update is invariant to a fixed positive rescaling of each gradient coordinate across
      the entire history when ϵ = 0, and approximately so when ϵ is negligible (Kingma & Ba, 2015). This
153
      property does not guarantee invariance to domain reweighting, which can change gradient direction,
154
      or to changing only the current gradient while holding saved moments fixed. Our comparisons
155   measure these effects empirically.
156
157   Different gradients produce similar Adam updates. On six fixed batches at joint-PG check-
158   points 100 and 250, I64 gradient cosines average 0.680 (DR–DT), 0.483 (DR–GT), and 0.822 (DT–
159   GT). Student weights, responses, masks, and teacher scores are shared, isolating loss normalization.
160   Applying the gradients to identical copies of the saved Adam moments raises FP32 update cosines
161   to 0.960, 0.939, and 0.979. Adam thus produces much more aligned updates from directionally
      different gradients.

246   4.1                   S PARSE BF16 CHANGES MASK WIDESPREAD FP32 UPDATES
247
248   For cumulative changes ∆ = θ − θ0 or individual steps u = θafter − θbefore , we measure the
                                     PP
249   changed fraction fτ (z) = P −1 j=1 1{|zj | > τ } and the smallest coordinate fraction carrying
250   90% of squared change. Here P is the number of coordinates; reported nonzero fractions use τ = 0.
251   Both metrics are computed for FP32 master weights and stored BF16 weights.
252   After 500 updates, joint PG changes 7.30% of BF16 weights, with 3.79% of coordinates carrying
253   90% of squared change; I64-DR gives 8.46% and 4.38% (Figure 3a). In FP32, however, 97.37%
254   and 97.17% of weights change, and the 90%-energy fractions rise to 34.03% and 35.14%. Even
255   the individual step at update 500 changes 86.80% and 87.05% of FP32 coordinates. Most param-
256   eters receive optimizer updates, many of which disappear in BF16. Sparse checkpoint differences
257   therefore do not imply updates restricted to a small parameter subset.
258   Similar scores also coexist with different cumulative changes: I64-DR/DT/GT change
259   8.46/10.52/11.24% of BF16 coordinates, with 4.38/5.39/5.92% carrying 90% of squared change.
260   A larger changed fraction does not imply better performance.

305
306   More vocabulary supervision need not spread parameter updates more widely. Sampled-token, top-
307   k, and full-vocabulary losses have nearly identical one-step concentration at the same student and
308   Adam state, consistent with concentrated changes under dense distillation (Yu et al., 2026). End-
309   to-end scores nevertheless differ: I64 improves mathematics over PG at the endpoint while losing

314

461
462   Distillation objectives and vocabulary truncation. MiniLLM uses reverse KL (Gu et al., 2024);
463   Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD) combines student-generated sequences with alterna-
464   tive divergences (Agarwal et al., 2024); f -DISTILL derives word-level losses from sequence-level
465
      f -divergences (Wen et al., 2023). Sparse Logit Sampling analyzes top-k caching bias (Anshu-
      mann et al., 2025), while Tail-Aware OPD restores tail-mass information lost by top-k renormal-
466
      ization (Huang & Wei, 2026). Our intersection loss retains full-softmax log ratios with detached
467
      student-top-k weights. Section 5 compares its coverage, gradient error, updates, and task scores
468   with sampled-token and full-vocabulary supervision.
469
470   Multi-teacher distillation and loss normalization. MOPD integrates specialized teachers on
471   student-generated responses (Ma et al., 2026); Open-MOPD adds domain token balancing and adap-
472   tive weighting (Gao et al., 2026). DAPO uses token-level averaging (Yu et al., 2025), and Dr. GRPO
473   analyzes length and reward-normalization bias (Liu et al., 2025). We separate domain weighting
474   from within-domain response weighting: Open-MOPD’s token-share correction gives DT for fixed
475   batches and domain weights, while DR also removes response-length weighting.
476
477   Post-training parameter geometry. Mukherjee et al. (2025) study small parameter subsets sup-
478   porting RL fine-tuning; Yu et al. (2026) report concentrated distillation updates and overlap between
479
      separately trained students. Shen et al. (2026) examine OPD geometry and response-position se-
      lection. We compare teachers at the same student and Adam state, distinguish FP32 from BF16
480
      changes, and vary vocabulary support at fixed prefixes.

1112   F                P ROMPT E XPOSURE AND C ANDIDATE -S ET W EIGHTS
1113
1114   When each domain loss is averaged separately, its prompt quota and loss weight control different
1115   quantities. Increasing the quota at fixed λd changes the number of sampled responses and the vari-
1116   ance of the gradient estimate, without directly increasing the domain’s expected contribution to the
1117   total gradient. Mathematics-only runs receive four times as many mathematics prompts per update
1118   as joint runs. Their score differences therefore cannot be attributed to teacher count alone.
1119   Candidate-set coverage also scales the distillation gradient. For the student candidate set S and its
1120   intersection I with the teacher set in Equation 3, the retained student weights sum to r(h) = pθ (I |
1121   h)/pθ (S | h). At a fixed prefix with a nonempty intersection and detached coefficients, the I64
1122   gradient equals r(h) times the gradient obtained by renormalizing the same weights over I. Equal
1123   response weights therefore do not imply equal gradient scale at every prefix. Measuring the retained
1124   mass and empty-intersection rate by domain can reveal unequal coverage under a fixed candidate
1125   size. Enlarging the candidate set or renormalizing its intersection changes the objective and requires
1126
       a separate performance comparison.

       H.2   F ROZEN SUBSETS AND GRADIENT COMPARISONS
1440
1441   Within each domain we select representative prefixes, prefixes above its 75th entropy percentile,
1442   and prefixes with I64 mass below 0.99. Selection precedes the objective-gradient comparison, al-
1443   lows overlap between strata, and uses up to 32 distinct prompts per domain. The low-coverage code
1444   stratum has only 21 eligible prompts, giving 85 prefixes overall rather than 96; no duplicated posi-
1445   tions fill the gap. Each stratum is divided into three batches. Small candidate budgets k = 4, 8 are
1446   selected by a coverage-only rule before inspecting objective gradients.
1447   The BF16 coverage audit sorts a top-128 list and takes its first k entries, whereas gradient diagnostics
1448   recompute top-k directly in FP32 at the exported BF16 parameter values. Student forward/backward
1449   passes use FP32; cached teacher targets use BF16 forward passes followed by FP32 log-softmax.
1450   Thus their probabilities and threshold membership need not match bit for bit; one of the 85 frozen
1451   low-coverage prefixes crosses 0.99 during the FP32 diagnostic, and remains in its original stratum.
1452   The FP32 diagnostic mean I64 masses are 99.049%, 95.684%, and 93.121% for the representative,
1453   high-entropy, and low-coverage groups. These are domain-weighted summaries of the selected
1454
       prefixes, not estimates of their prevalence in natural generation.
1455   Full KL, PG, and intersection objectives use the definitions in Appendix D. Empty intersections
1456   produce zero loss. We retain zero- and small-norm cases and report vector differences in addition
1457   to cosine. The plotted error is ∥gIk − gfull ∥/∥gfull ∥, not the difference of the two gradient norms.
